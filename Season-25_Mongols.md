# EPISODE 86 — THE STORM OUT OF THE STEPPES

## The Story

Just as the ummah, under Salahuddin and his heirs, had begun to recover its footing in the west against the Crusaders, a force was rising in the far north-east that would make the Crusades look like a border skirmish. It came from the vast grasslands of **Mongolia** — and it would become the largest contiguous land empire the world has ever known.

Its architect was a steppe chieftain who united the warring nomadic tribes under one banner and took the title **Genghis Khan** (Chinggis Khan). The Mongols were unlike any enemy the Muslim world had faced: superb horsemen, masters of mobility and terror, organised with ruthless discipline, and willing to annihilate entire cities to break the will of all the rest. Their strategy was psychological as much as military — make an example so horrifying that the next city surrenders without a fight.

The catastrophe that struck the eastern Muslim world began, by the accounts, with a **diplomatic disaster.** Around **615–616 AH / 1218–1219 CE**, Genghis Khan sought trade with the powerful Muslim empire to his west — the **Khwarazmian Empire**, which ruled Persia and Central Asia. He sent a caravan of merchants and envoys. The governor of a Khwarazmian frontier city, suspecting them as spies, **seized the caravan and had the merchants killed.** When Genghis Khan sent envoys to demand justice, the Khwarazmshah had **them** killed or humiliated too.

It was one of the most catastrophic decisions in history. The killing of envoys — a violation of a sacred custom — unleashed the full fury of the Mongol war machine on the Muslim east.

What followed (from around 617 AH / 1220 CE) was almost beyond description. The great, glittering cities of Central Asia and Persia — **Bukhara, Samarkand, Merv, Nishapur, Herat** — cities that had been among the brightest centres of Islamic learning on earth (this was the homeland of Imam al-Bukhari himself) — were stormed and, in many cases, **systematically destroyed**, their populations slaughtered or enslaved, their libraries and mosques burned. The chronicler **Ibn al-Athir**, who lived through the news of it, wrote that he wished he had died before witnessing it, calling it a calamity the like of which had not befallen humanity since the creation of Adam — and confessed that for years he could not bring himself to write of it at all. The numbers the sources give are staggering and surely exaggerated, but the scale of the devastation was real and unprecedented.

The Khwarazmian Empire — one of the most powerful Muslim states of its day — simply **ceased to exist.** And the Mongol storm did not stop. Over the following decades, under Genghis Khan's sons and grandsons, the Mongols pushed ever westward, swallowing kingdom after kingdom, until they stood, at last, at the gates of the symbolic heart of the entire Muslim world: **Baghdad.**

## The Qur'an Says

- **Surah Al-Baqarah (2:155–156)** — "*And We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger and a loss of wealth and lives and fruits, but give good tidings to the patient — who, when disaster strikes them, say, 'Indeed we belong to Allah, and to Him we will return.'*" — the only words adequate to a catastrophe of this scale. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Aal-e-Imran (3:140)** — "*...And these days [of varying fortunes] We alternate among the people so that Allah may make evident those who believe...*" — the divine wisdom that fortunes rise and fall, and that calamity sifts the believers. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Al-Anfal (8:25)** — "*And fear a trial (fitnah) which will not strike those who have wronged among you exclusively...*" — the way a catastrophe engulfs whole societies, the just with the unjust. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Ar-Rum (30:41)** — "*Corruption has appeared on land and sea because of what the hands of people have earned...*" — the Qur'anic lens on how human wrongs and disunity invite ruin. ✓ Qur'an.

## The Authentic Hadith

- **A people who will be a great trial — interpreted, with caution, as the Turks/Mongols:** The Prophet ﷺ described, among the signs and trials, fighting a people *"with small eyes, broad faces, whose faces are like hammered shields, who wear shoes of hair."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ as a sound hadith; ⚠️ its *identification* specifically with the Mongols is an **interpretation** many scholars made, not part of the text itself — we note it as interpretation, not certainty.

- **Patience at the first shock:** *"Indeed, patience is at the first strike of calamity."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari.** ✓ The believer's response to sudden catastrophe.

- **No relief without trial first:** *"...and that victory comes with patience, and that relief comes with distress, and that with hardship comes ease."* — **Musnad Ahmad / Tirmidhi** (hasan). ✓ The promise that even this darkness would pass.

- **The sanctity of the diplomatic custom:** The Prophet ﷺ held envoys inviolable; he said to two messengers (of Musaylimah) that were it not that envoys are not killed, he would have struck their necks. — **Musnad Ahmad / Abu Dawud** (sound in meaning). ✓ Underlining how catastrophic the Khwarazmian killing of envoys was — a violation that helped unleash the storm.

## Historical / Theological Context

**Scale and accuracy:** The Mongol conquests were genuinely among the deadliest events in human history, and the destruction of the Central Asian and Persian cities was catastrophic and well-documented. *However*, the specific casualty figures in the medieval chronicles (millions killed in a single city, etc.) are widely regarded by modern historians as **exaggerated** — partly Mongol psychological warfare, partly the shock of traumatised witnesses. We affirm the catastrophe while flagging the numbers as unreliable.

**Why it happened — the believing and historical readings together:** Historically, the Muslim east was politically fragmented and the Khwarazmian Empire was internally weak and newly-built; the murder of the Mongol envoys provided the trigger. The believing reading does not contradict this but adds the recurring Qur'anic theme of this whole arc: **disunity and the wrongs of rulers leave a people exposed** (8:25; 30:41). As in al-Andalus and as before the Crusades, a divided ummah could not withstand a united enemy.

**Not a demonisation of a people:** The Mongols were not uniquely evil monsters; they were a people whose military culture made war total and terrifying. Crucially — as Episode 88 shows — within two generations many Mongols **embraced Islam** and became patrons of the very civilisation their grandfathers had ravaged. History is not a story of permanent villains; it is a story of human beings, and of Allah's power to change hearts.

**Sunni–Shia note (respectful):** The catastrophe fell upon the eastern Muslim world broadly. We narrate it without sectarian blame and **insult no people or nation,** including the Mongols.

## Weak & Disputed Narrations

- ⚠️ The **casualty figures** for the destroyed cities (often in the hundreds of thousands or millions per city) are almost certainly **inflated**; the devastation was immense but the precise numbers are not reliable.

- ⚠️ The identification of specific hadith about a wide-faced, small-eyed people **with the Mongols specifically** is a **scholarly interpretation**, not an explicit statement of the text. Present it as such, not as a settled fact.

- ⚠️ Dramatic individual atrocity-anecdotes from the chronicles, while reflecting a real horror, often carry the marks of trauma and propaganda; treat specific lurid details with caution.

## What We Don't Know

- ❓ The true death tolls — genuinely unknown and almost certainly far lower than the chronicles claim, though still catastrophic.
- ❓ The full internal politics of the Khwarazmian collapse.
- ❓ The precise sequence and details of many of the Central Asian sieges.

## The Lessons

1. **A small injustice by a leader can unleash a vast catastrophe on a people.** The murder of envoys — a single reckless act — helped bring down an empire. Those in authority carry consequences far beyond themselves; recklessness at the top becomes ruin at the bottom.
2. **"Patience is at the first strike."** The Qur'an and Sunnah do not promise believers a life without catastrophe; they promise that *"to Allah we belong and to Him we return"* (2:156) is enough to stand on, even when the world is ending.
3. **Disunity is, once again, the open gate.** A third time in this arc — al-Andalus, the Crusades, and now the Mongols — a fragmented ummah falls to a united foe. The lesson refuses to stop repeating until it is learned.
4. **No darkness in history is the final word.** Even the worst calamity is a chapter, not the conclusion. Hold on — relief comes after distress, and Allah's plan is longer than any catastrophe.

**→ Next:** The storm reaches Baghdad — the City of Peace, the heart of the golden age, the seat of the caliphate for five centuries. And what happens there in 1258 will break the heart of the ummah.

# EPISODE 87 — THE FALL OF BAGHDAD

## The Story

In **656 AH / 1258 CE**, the Mongol storm reached the city that had stood, for five hundred years, as the symbolic heart of the Muslim world: **Baghdad** — the City of Peace, the home of the House of Wisdom, the seat of the Abbasid Caliphate since al-Mansur founded it (Season 22).

The Mongol army was led by **Hulagu Khan**, a grandson of Genghis Khan, at the head of one of the largest forces the Mongols had ever assembled. The reigning Abbasid caliph, **al-Musta'sim** — the thirty-seventh and, as it would turn out, the **last** Abbasid caliph in Baghdad — was, by most accounts, a weak and poorly-advised ruler. The Muslim world was, yet again, disastrously divided, and no united relief came. Negotiations failed; the caliph neither submitted in time nor prepared an adequate defence.

The siege was short and the end was apocalyptic. The walls were breached, and what followed was, by the testimony of the sources, a horror almost beyond the capacity of words. For days, the Mongols **sacked Baghdad** — the killing was on a massive scale, the caliph was executed, and the accumulated treasures of five centuries of the golden age were plundered or destroyed.

And then came the loss that scholars have mourned ever since: the **destruction of the libraries.** The great repositories of Baghdad — the inheritance of the House of Wisdom, countless irreplaceable manuscripts of science, medicine, philosophy, literature, theology, and law, the accumulated learning of half a millennium — were **destroyed.** The chronicles relate that so many books were thrown into the **Tigris River** that its waters were said to run **black with the ink** that bled from them. Whether literally true or a haunting image of a real catastrophe, it captures an immeasurable loss to human civilisation. Knowledge that had taken centuries to gather was gone in days.

With the death of al-Musta'sim, the **Abbasid Caliphate in Baghdad came to an end** — a line that reached back five hundred years, and whose symbolic authority had connected, however thinly, to the early days of Islam. For the first time since the death of the Prophet ﷺ, the Muslim world had **no caliph at all.** It felt, to those who lived through it, like the end of the world.

The Mongols pressed on toward Syria, taking Aleppo and Damascus. Ahead of them lay Egypt — the last great independent Muslim power in the region, ruled now by a remarkable new dynasty. It seemed only a matter of time before it, too, fell, and the entire heartland of Islam passed under Mongol rule.

The ummah had never been lower. And it was precisely here, at the very bottom, that the impossible happened.

## The Qur'an Says

- **Surah Al-Baqarah (2:156)** — "*...Who, when disaster strikes them, say, 'Indeed we belong to Allah, and indeed to Him we will return.'*" — the words on the lips of a people watching their world burn. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Aal-e-Imran (3:26)** — "*Say, 'O Allah, Owner of Sovereignty, You give sovereignty to whom You will and You take sovereignty away from whom You will...'*" — the truth laid bare by the fall of a five-hundred-year caliphate: all dominion is Allah's, given and withdrawn by Him alone. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Al-Hadid (57:20)** — "*Know that the life of this world is but amusement and diversion... and the life of this world is nothing but the enjoyment of delusion.*" — the impermanence of even the mightiest worldly glory. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Al-Hijr (15:9)** — "*Indeed, it is We who sent down the Qur'an and indeed, We will be its guardian.*" — even as libraries burned, the one Book that could never be destroyed remained, preserved in a million hearts (the promise of Season 20). ✓ Qur'an.

## The Authentic Hadith

- **Sovereignty rises and falls by Allah's decree:** consistent with the Prophet's ﷺ teaching that *the caliphate on the prophetic method lasts thirty years, then comes kingship* (**Tirmidhi**, sahih) — worldly rule was always foretold to be a changing, mortal thing. ✓

- **Knowledge is taken by the death of the scholars:** *"Allah does not take away knowledge by snatching it from people, but He takes it away by the death of the scholars, until none remains..."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ A chilling resonance with the loss of Baghdad's scholars and books — and a reminder that the gravest loss is the loss of *people* of knowledge.

- **Patience at the shock of calamity:** *"Patience is at the first strike of calamity."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari.** ✓

- **The preserved religion endures:** *"A group of my ummah will remain upon the truth, victorious... until the command of Allah comes."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ The promise that the deen itself would survive even this.

## Historical / Theological Context

**The end of an era:** The fall of Baghdad in 1258 is one of the great turning points of world history — conventionally marking the end of the Islamic "Golden Age" centred on Baghdad and the end of the Abbasid Caliphate as a real political institution. (A shadow Abbasid line would later be hosted by the Mamluks in Cairo as a purely symbolic figurehead, and centuries later the title would pass, in a different form, to the Ottomans — threads for a future arc.)

**The loss of knowledge — and what could not be lost:** The destruction of Baghdad's libraries was a civilisational catastrophe; an unknowable wealth of human learning vanished. Yet two profound consolations stand out. First, much Islamic knowledge had *already* been copied and dispersed across the Muslim world — to Cairo, Damascus, al-Andalus, India — so the tradition survived even though Baghdad did not. Second, and most importantly, the **Qur'an could not be burned out of existence**: preserved in the hearts of countless *huffaz* and in copies everywhere (Season 20, 15:9), it stood untouched by the flames that consumed everything else. The libraries proved mortal; the revelation did not.

**A theological reckoning:** For the believers, the fall of Baghdad was a shattering test that forced a return to first principles — that *all* sovereignty belongs to Allah (3:26), that worldly glory is fleeting (57:20), and that the ummah's security had never truly rested in a city or a throne, but in its faith and its unity. The catastrophe was, in the hardest possible way, a summons back to Allah.

**Sunni–Shia note (respectful):** Some partisan later narratives sought to assign blame for Baghdad's fall to one sect or another (e.g., accusations against the caliph's minister Ibn al-Alqami, or claims about the scholar Nasir al-Din al-Tusi who was in Hulagu's service). These blame-narratives are **historically disputed and heavily coloured by sectarian polemic.** Responsible scholarship treats them with great caution. We decline to assign sectarian blame, note that the catastrophe fell upon all, and **insult no group.**

## Weak & Disputed Narrations

- ⚠️ The **casualty figures** for Baghdad (often given as hundreds of thousands to over a million) are **disputed and likely exaggerated**; the catastrophe was immense, the precise numbers unknowable.

- ⚠️ The image of the **Tigris running black with ink** (and red with blood) is a powerful and oft-repeated tradition; it may be literal, literary, or both — we present it as the *symbol of a real and immeasurable loss*, not as a verified statistic.

- ⚠️ **Sectarian blame-narratives** (Ibn al-Alqami, al-Tusi, etc.) for the fall are **contested and polemical**; do not present them as established fact.

## What We Don't Know

- ❓ The true death toll and the full extent of what was lost in the libraries.
- ❓ The precise roles and motives of the various advisors around both the caliph and Hulagu.
- ❓ How much knowledge was permanently lost versus preserved in copies elsewhere — a genuinely open question.

## The Lessons

1. **All sovereignty is a loan from Allah.** A caliphate of five centuries ended in days. "*You give sovereignty to whom You will and take it from whom You will*" (3:26). Never mistake any worldly institution, however ancient, for something permanent.
2. **The gravest loss is the loss of people of knowledge.** Books can be recopied; a generation of scholars, once gone, takes its understanding with it. Honour, protect, and *become* people of beneficial knowledge.
3. **What is anchored only in this world can be burned; what is anchored in Allah cannot.** The libraries fell; the Qur'an in the hearts of the believers did not. Build your security on what no enemy can reach.
4. **The lowest point is not the end.** Standing in the ashes of Baghdad, the ummah seemed finished. It was, in fact, on the very eve of one of the most stunning reversals in its history.

# EPISODE 88 — AYN JALUT AND THE LIGHT THAT WOULD NOT DIE

## The Story

After Baghdad, the Mongols seemed truly invincible. No army had stopped them — not the Persians, not the Khwarazmians, not the Abbasids. They had crossed half the world leaving ruin behind them. Now, under the general **Kitbuqa**, a Mongol army swept into Syria, took Damascus, and sent envoys to **Egypt** demanding its surrender. Egypt was the last great Muslim power standing between the Mongols and the total conquest of the Islamic heartland.

Egypt was ruled by an extraordinary new dynasty: the **Mamluks.** The word *mamluk* means "owned" — they had been **slave-soldiers**, boys (largely of Turkic and Circassian origin) purchased, raised, converted to Islam, and trained into an elite warrior caste, who had then seized power for themselves and founded a state. These former slaves were now the rulers of Egypt — and they were about to do what no one on earth had managed.

When the Mongol envoys arrived demanding submission, the Mamluk sultan, **Sayf ad-Din Qutuz**, made his choice: he would fight. By the well-known accounts he had the envoys executed — a declaration that there would be no surrender — and resolved to march out and meet the Mongols in open battle rather than wait to be besieged. Standing with him was a brilliant and ferocious commander, a former slave himself, named **Baybars** (Rukn ad-Din Baybars).

The two armies met on **25 Ramadan 658 AH / 3 September 1260 CE**, at a spring in the Galilee called **Ayn Jalut — "the Spring (or Pool) of Goliath,"** by tradition near where the Prophet Dawud (A.S.) had felled Goliath ages before. The symbolism was not lost on anyone: here, a seemingly small power would face a seemingly invincible giant.

Qutuz and Baybars fought with a discipline and courage the Mongols had rarely faced. By the accounts, the Mamluks used a feigned retreat to draw the Mongols into a trap, then enveloped and crushed them. When part of the Muslim line wavered under the Mongol assault, Qutuz is said to have thrown his helmet to the ground so his face could be seen, and cried out **"Wa Islamah!" — "O [for the sake of] Islam!"** — rallying his men. The Mongol army was broken. Their general Kitbuqa was killed. For the **first time in their westward march, the Mongols had been decisively defeated in open battle.**

**Ayn Jalut** is one of the most consequential battles in world history. It shattered the myth of Mongol invincibility, halted their advance into the heart of the Muslim world and into Africa, and saved Egypt — which now became the great refuge and rebuilding-centre of Islamic civilisation, preserving and continuing the learning that Baghdad had lost. The light had been on the verge of extinction; at a desert spring, it was saved.

And then came the most beautiful turn of all — the turn that forbids us from ever telling this as a tale of permanent enemies. **The Mongols, in time, embraced Islam.** Branch by branch, the descendants of the conquerors became Muslims. In the north, **Berke Khan**, a grandson of Genghis Khan and ruler of the Golden Horde, accepted Islam and even turned against his own Mongol kin over the sack of Baghdad. In Persia, the Mongol Ilkhanate's ruler **Ghazan Khan** embraced Islam around **694 AH / 1295 CE**, and his realm became a Muslim state — patronising mosques, scholars, and the arts. Within a few generations, the grandchildren of the men who burned Baghdad's libraries were building libraries, endowing madrasas, and memorising the Qur'an. The faith that the Mongols had tried to drown had, instead, **conquered the conquerors** — not by the sword, but by the truth and beauty of the deen itself.

This is the deepest lesson of Arc X, and a fitting place to pause the journey. Empires rose and fell — Umayyad, Abbasid, Andalusi, Ayyubid, Mamluk. Catastrophes came that seemed to end everything — the Crusades, the Mongols, the fall of Baghdad. And yet through all of it, the **light never went out.** It was preserved in the Qur'an that could not be burned, in the hearts of the *huffaz*, in the scholars who fled and rebuilt, in the slave-soldiers who made their stand, and — astonishingly — it spread even into the hearts of those who had come to destroy it. The same unbroken light that the Companions carried out of Madinah, that Abd al-Rahman planted in Spain, that Salahuddin guarded in Jerusalem, survived the worst the world could throw at it.

Because Allah had promised to preserve it — and Allah keeps His promises.

## The Qur'an Says

- **Surah Al-Baqarah (2:249)** — "*...How many a small company has overcome a large company by permission of Allah. And Allah is with the patient.*" — the very principle of Ayn Jalut: the smaller, patient force overcoming the giant by Allah's leave. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Al-Baqarah (2:251)** — "*...And Dawud killed Goliath (Jalut), and Allah gave him the kingship and wisdom...*" — the original "Goliath" felled near the very spring (*Ayn Jalut*) where the Mongol giant was now broken. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah As-Saff (61:8)** — "*They want to extinguish the light of Allah with their mouths, but Allah will perfect His light, although the disbelievers dislike it.*" — the promise fulfilled across this entire arc: the light could not be extinguished. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Al-Hijr (15:9)** — "*Indeed, it is We who sent down the Qur'an and indeed, We will be its guardian.*" — the guarantee that held firm through every catastrophe. ✓ Qur'an.

## The Authentic Hadith

- **Victory with patience, the small overcoming the large:** *"...victory comes with patience..."* — **Musnad Ahmad / Tirmidhi** (hasan) — embodied at Ayn Jalut. ✓

- **The victorious group that endures:** *"A group of my ummah will remain steadfast upon the truth, victorious, unharmed by those who oppose them, until the command of Allah comes."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ The deen's survival, against all odds, made visible.

- **Allah renews the religion:** *"Allah will send to this ummah at the head of every hundred years one who will renew for it its religion."* — **Sunan Abu Dawud**, **sahih/hasan.** ✓ The principle of *tajdid* (renewal) that carried the ummah through and beyond the catastrophe — and indeed, this era produced great renewers and scholars who rebuilt the tradition.

- **Hearts in Allah's hand:** *"The hearts of the children of Adam are all between two of the fingers of the Most Merciful, as one heart; He turns it however He wills."* — **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ The hadith that explains the unexplainable: how the hearts of the conquering Mongols were turned to the faith of the conquered.

## Historical / Theological Context

**Ayn Jalut's significance:** Historians rank it among history's decisive battles. It was the first major open-field defeat of the Mongols, it permanently checked their expansion into Egypt and Africa, and it established the **Mamluk Sultanate** as the dominant power of the region and the great protector of Islamic civilisation for the next two and a half centuries. Baybars would soon become sultan and a formidable ruler who also rolled back the remaining Crusader states. Egypt (Cairo) became the new heart of Sunni learning, inheriting the role Baghdad had lost.

**The conversion of the Mongols — Allah's astonishing reversal:** This is one of history's most remarkable phenomena. The conquerors were, within generations, absorbed into the faith and civilisation of those they had conquered. The Golden Horde (under Berke and later Uzbeg Khan), the Ilkhanate (under Ghazan), and the Chagatai Mongols largely became Muslim; from these Islamised Turco-Mongol roots would later grow great Muslim empires (such as the Mughals of India — a future arc). The sword that fell on Islam became, in time, a sword *for* it. This is the living proof of the hadith that hearts are between Allah's fingers, turned however He wills.

**A balanced, non-vengeful telling:** We have refused throughout to demonise the Mongols as a people. They did terrible things in war; they were also human beings whose descendants became devout Muslims, scholars, and patrons. History, told with the eyes of faith, is never a permanent ledger of enemies — it is the arena of Allah's mercy and the turning of hearts.

**Sunni–Shia note (respectful):** The Mamluks were Sunni; the Islamised Mongol states included both Sunni and (later, in some regions) Shia developments. We present this within the series' Sunni framing and **insult no people or sect,** including the Mongols, whom Allah guided in such numbers.

## Weak & Disputed Narrations

- ⚠️ The dramatic battle details of Ayn Jalut (Qutuz throwing down his helmet, the exact cry of "Wa Islamah!", the precise feigned-retreat tactics) come from chronicle traditions of varying reliability; the *outcome and significance* are historically certain, the cinematic detail less so.

- ⚠️ The execution of the Mongol envoys by Qutuz is widely reported but, like all such accounts, carries some uncertainty in its details.

- ⚠️ Pious legends later attached to the Mongol conversions (miraculous dreams, single dramatic conversion moments) are often **simplified**; the real process was gradual, political, and spiritual at once. The *fact* of large-scale conversion is certain.

## What We Don't Know

- ❓ The exact size of the armies at Ayn Jalut (the Mongol force there was likely smaller than their full strength, as the bulk had withdrawn east — a factor historians weigh).
- ❓ The precise tactical details of the battle.
- ❓ The full inner story of why and how the Mongol conversions unfolded as they did — visible in outline, mysterious in its depths, known fully only to Allah.

## The Lessons

1. **The small and patient can overcome the giant — by Allah's permission.** "*How many a small company has overcome a large company by the permission of Allah*" (2:249). Ayn Jalut is that verse written in history. Never measure outcomes only by the visible odds.
2. **Allah's light cannot be extinguished.** They tried — Crusaders, Mongols, the flames of Baghdad — and "*Allah will perfect His light*" (61:8). The survival of the deen through this arc is not an accident of history; it is a promise of God being kept.
3. **Hearts are in Allah's hand — even your enemy's.** The conquerors became believers. No one is beyond guidance; no enemy is permanent. Make du'a even for those who oppose you; their hearts are between the fingers of the Most Merciful.
4. **From the lowest point, Allah can raise the highest deliverance.** The ashes of Baghdad gave way, in two short years, to the triumph of Ayn Jalut and, in two generations, to the conversion of the Mongols. When you are at the very bottom, remember Whose hand writes the next chapter.

## ✓ SEASON 25 COMPLETE

**What we witnessed:** The greatest catastrophe and one of the greatest recoveries in Islamic history. Out of the steppes came the **Mongols**, who shattered the Khwarazmian Empire and the luminous cities of Central Asia, and in **1258 sacked Baghdad** — ending the Abbasid Caliphate, drowning five centuries of learning, and leaving the ummah, for the first time, with no caliph and no apparent hope. And then, at **Ayn Jalut (1260)**, the slave-soldiers of Egypt — the **Mamluks**, under **Qutuz and Baybars** — did what no one had done: they broke the Mongols in open battle and saved the heartland of Islam. Most wondrous of all, the conquerors themselves — Berke, Ghazan, and so many others — **embraced the faith they had come to destroy.** The light that could not be burned could not be conquered either.

**The thread / theme:** *Empires die and catastrophes come, but Allah's light cannot be extinguished — He saves it through the patient few, and He turns the very hearts of its enemies toward it.*
