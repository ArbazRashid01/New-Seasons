# EPISODE 83 — THE FALL OF JERUSALEM

## The Story

While al-Andalus was splintering in the far west (Season 23), a storm broke over the eastern Mediterranean that would scar the Muslim world for two centuries.

In **489 AH / 1096 CE**, vast armies set out from Western Europe, summoned by a papal call, marked with the cross — the **Crusaders** (the Muslims called them *al-Faranj*, "the Franks"). Their stated goal was to seize the Holy Land and, above all, **Jerusalem** — the city sacred to Christians, Jews, and Muslims alike; the city of the Prophet's ﷺ Night Journey (*al-Isra*); the site of Masjid al-Aqsa, the first *qiblah* toward which the Muslims had prayed.

They arrived at a catastrophic moment for Muslim unity. The eastern Muslim world was **fragmented** — the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad was by now largely a figurehead under the control of others; the Seljuk Turks were divided among quarrelling princes; and **Egypt was ruled by the Fatimids**, a Shia Isma'ili dynasty often at odds with the Sunni powers of Syria. Into this disunity the Crusaders marched, and the disunity let them through. (The lesson of al-Andalus — *disunity destroys what enemies cannot* — was being written again, simultaneously, at the other end of the Muslim world.)

The Crusaders took Antioch, then pushed south. And on a July day in **492 AH / 1099 CE**, after a brutal siege, they breached the walls of **Jerusalem.**

What followed was a massacre that horrified even some who recorded it. When the Crusaders took the city, they slaughtered its inhabitants — Muslims and Jews together — in numbers the chronicles describe in terrible terms; the killing is said to have filled the streets around the sacred precinct. The Jews of the city, who had taken refuge in their synagogue, perished. Masjid al-Aqsa itself, where the Prophet ﷺ had led the prophets in prayer on the night of the Mi'raj, was turned into a site of slaughter and then converted to other uses. The blessed city the Qur'an honours had fallen, and fallen in blood.

The Crusaders established their states along the coast — the Kingdom of Jerusalem and others — and for nearly a century they held the heart of the Holy Land. For the Muslim world, the loss of Jerusalem was a wound of the deepest kind: not merely a strategic defeat, but the seizure of one of the three holiest places on earth. And yet, in their disunity, the Muslim rulers spent years failing to mount a serious, united response.

The recovery, when it finally came, would require something the ummah had lost: **unity** — and a leader with the vision to forge it and the character to deserve it.

## The Qur'an Says

- **Surah Al-Isra (17:1)** — "*Exalted is He who took His Servant by night from al-Masjid al-Haram to al-Masjid al-Aqsa, whose surroundings We have blessed, to show him of Our signs...*" — the Qur'anic sanctity of the very city that had fallen, the site of the Night Journey. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Al-Baqarah (2:155–157)** — "*And We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger and a loss of wealth and lives and fruits, but give good tidings to the patient — who, when disaster strikes them, say, 'Indeed we belong to Allah and to Him we will return.'*" — the believer's response to catastrophe. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Aal-e-Imran (3:139)** — "*So do not weaken and do not grieve, for you will be superior if you are [true] believers.*" — the verse of hope for a defeated and demoralised ummah. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Al-Anfal (8:46)** — "*...and do not dispute, lest you lose courage and your strength depart...*" — the disunity that opened Jerusalem's gates, just as it had opened Cordoba's. ✓ Qur'an.

## The Authentic Hadith

- **The sanctity of Jerusalem (al-Aqsa):** *"Do not undertake a journey except to three mosques: al-Masjid al-Haram, this mosque of mine, and al-Masjid al-Aqsa."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ The rank of the city the Crusaders seized.

- **Al-Aqsa, the second mosque on earth:** Abu Dharr (R.A.) asked which mosque was built first; the Prophet ﷺ said al-Masjid al-Haram, then al-Masjid al-Aqsa, forty years apart. — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓

- **The sanctity of innocent life:** *"Whoever kills a person protected by a treaty (mu'ahad) will not smell the fragrance of Paradise..."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari.** ✓ The standard by which *all* killing of non-combatants — by any side — is condemned.

- **A group remains on truth:** *"A group of my ummah will remain victorious upon the truth... until the command of Allah comes."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ The hope that resistance and revival were always possible, even after Jerusalem fell.

## Historical / Theological Context

**What the Crusades were:** A series of religiously-sanctioned military campaigns (traditionally numbered, beginning with the First Crusade of 1096–1099) launched from Western Europe, primarily to seize and hold the Holy Land. They were driven by a mix of genuine religious fervour, papal politics, and the ambitions of European nobility. They are *medieval* events; reading them as a verdict on Christianity-as-such, or on Christians today, is a historical and moral error this series rejects.

**The 1099 massacre — and honesty about violence:** The slaughter at the fall of Jerusalem in 1099 is well-documented, including in Crusader sources that recorded it with grim pride. We state it plainly. We will *also* state plainly, in Episode 85, the starkly different conduct of Salahuddin when he retook the city — not to score points, but because the contrast is the moral heart of this season. And we hold the universal standard: the killing of non-combatants is condemned **whoever** does it.

**Why Jerusalem matters so deeply in Islam:** It is the city of the Night Journey (17:1), the location of al-Masjid al-Aqsa (the first qiblah, the third holiest site in Islam), and a city woven through the stories of the prophets the whole series has followed — Dawud, Sulayman, Isa (peace be upon them). Its loss was felt as the loss of a sacred trust.

**Sunni–Shia note (respectful):** The political landscape that let the Crusaders in included the Sunni–Shia divide of the age (Sunni Syria vs. Fatimid Shia Egypt). We narrate this as a *political* fragmentation that weakened the Muslim response, without sectarian insult. The eventual reunification under Salahuddin would bring Egypt and Syria together — a story we tell with respect to all. We **insult no one.**

## Weak & Disputed Narrations

- ⚠️ The exact death toll of the 1099 massacre is debated by historians; the figures in the medieval chronicles (Muslim and Crusader alike) are likely **exaggerated**, but that a large-scale massacre occurred is not in doubt.

- ⚠️ Apocalyptic and eschatological hadith sometimes attached to the Crusades and to Jerusalem (the great *malahim*/end-times battles) require careful, qualified handling; many are sound texts whose *application* to specific historical events is interpretive. Do not map them onto history casually. ⚠️

- ✗ Any use of this history to incite hatred against Christians today, or to justify violence against innocents, is **rejected** — it contradicts the Qur'an (60:8) and the explicit example of Salahuddin.

## What We Don't Know

- ❓ Precise casualty figures for the fall of Jerusalem and the campaigns.
- ❓ The exact internal calculations of the divided Muslim rulers who failed to respond for so long.
- ❓ Many specifics of life in the Crusader states for their Muslim, Jewish, and Eastern-Christian subjects (it varied widely by place and period).

## The Lessons

1. **Disunity is the open gate.** As in al-Andalus, so in the Levant: the enemy did not break a strong, united ummah — it walked through the gaps that division had left. The same Qur'anic warning (8:46) was fulfilled twice, at once, on opposite ends of the world.
2. **Loss is a test, not a verdict.** The fall of Jerusalem felt like the end. The Qur'an reframes such moments: "*Do not weaken and do not grieve*" (3:139). Defeat is a chapter, not the book.
3. **Hold the moral line even when others don't.** The 1099 massacre is a permanent reminder that the killing of innocents is a horror — and the believer's standard does not bend based on what an enemy does. Mercy is not weakness; it is principle.
4. **Sacred trusts demand united effort.** Recovering what matters most required the ummah to stop quarrelling and become one. Some goals are simply unreachable while we are divided.

**→ Next:** For decades the ummah bleeds and squabbles — until two reformers, Zengi and Nur ad-Din, begin stitching it back together, and a young Kurd named Yusuf is pulled, reluctantly, toward his destiny.

# EPISODE 84 — THE GATHERING OF THE PIECES

## The Story

The recovery of Jerusalem did not happen by a miracle or a single battle. It took the better part of a century of patient, unglamorous work: the work of **reuniting a fractured ummah.** And it was carried out by a succession of remarkable men, each preparing the ground for the next.

The first was **Imad ad-Din Zengi**, the Turkic ruler of Mosul and Aleppo, who in **539 AH / 1144 CE** recaptured **Edessa**, the first major Crusader state to fall — the first real sign that the tide could turn. His response to the Crusader presence was to begin uniting the squabbling Muslim cities of the north under one strong hand.

After Zengi came his son, **Nur ad-Din Mahmud Zangi** — and here the story deepens, because Nur ad-Din was not merely a conqueror; he was a man of profound piety and justice who understood that the war for Jerusalem was, first, a war for the **moral and spiritual revival of the ummah.** He united Syria, governed with famous justice, built mosques, schools (*madrasas*), and hospitals, revived Sunni learning, and lived with an austerity that shamed kings. He made the *reconquest of Jerusalem* a conscious, declared goal — he even had a magnificent *minbar* (pulpit) constructed in Aleppo, intending that it would one day be placed in a liberated al-Aqsa. He prepared the ummah's heart, not just its armies.

The final piece was **Egypt.** Egypt was still ruled by the **Fatimid** dynasty (Shia Isma'ili), now weak and unstable, and it became the object of a three-way struggle between the Crusaders, who coveted its wealth, and Nur ad-Din, who knew that **whoever united Egypt with Syria would have the Crusaders caught between two jaws.** Nur ad-Din sent expeditions to Egypt under a trusted general named **Shirkuh** — and Shirkuh brought along his young nephew.

That nephew was **Yusuf ibn Ayyub**, a Kurd, a thoughtful young man who, by the accounts, had no great ambition for war and even initially resisted being sent to Egypt. History would know him by his honorific: **Salah ad-Din — Salahuddin — "the Righteousness of the Faith."** Europe would call him **Saladin.**

Through a turn of events he had not sought, the young Yusuf found himself, after his uncle's death, the effective ruler of Egypt (around 564 AH / 1169 CE). He brought the Fatimid era to a peaceful end and **restored Egypt to Sunni Islam** and to allegiance with the wider Muslim world — and he did so, by most accounts, without the bloodshed such transitions usually brought. When Nur ad-Din died (569 AH / 1174 CE), Salahuddin gradually united **Egypt and Syria** under a single authority — founding the **Ayyubid** dynasty and assembling, at last, the unified power that the recovery of Jerusalem had always required.

The reluctant young Kurd had become the leader the ummah had been waiting for. And he had inherited from Nur ad-Din not only a strategy, but a *standard*: that the one who would liberate the holy city must first be worthy of it.

## The Qur'an Says

- **Surah Aal-e-Imran (3:103)** — "*And hold firmly to the rope of Allah all together and do not become divided, and remember the favour of Allah upon you — when you were enemies and He brought your hearts together...*" — the unification of a fractured ummah, the very work of Nur ad-Din and Salahuddin. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah As-Saff (61:4)** — "*Indeed, Allah loves those who fight in His cause in a row as though they are a [single] structure joined firmly.*" — the unity-as-one-structure that defeated division. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah An-Nur (24:55)** — "*Allah has promised those who have believed and done righteous deeds that He will surely grant them succession... and that He will surely establish for them their religion... and substitute security for them after their fear...*" — the promise of restoration to those who believe and act rightly. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Ar-Ra'd (13:11)** — "*...Allah will not change the condition of a people until they change what is in themselves...*" — Nur ad-Din's insight: revive the people within, and the victory without will follow. ✓ Qur'an.

## The Authentic Hadith

- **Justice of the ruler:** Among the seven shaded by Allah on the Day of Judgement is *"a just ruler."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ The standard Nur ad-Din and Salahuddin were measured against.

- **Building mosques and institutions:** *"Whoever builds a mosque for Allah, Allah builds for him a house in Paradise."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ Nur ad-Din's vast programme of mosques, schools, and hospitals.

- **Strength in unity:** *"The believer to the believer is like a building, each part strengthening the other."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ The principle behind uniting Egypt and Syria.

- **Intentions:** *"Actions are but by intentions, and every person will have only what they intended."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ The hadith that frames why *who* liberates Jerusalem, and *why*, mattered as much as the victory itself.

## Historical / Theological Context

**Revival before reconquest:** The genius of the Zengid–Ayyubid project, especially under Nur ad-Din, was the understanding that military victory had to rest on **internal revival** — justice, learning, sincere religion, and unity. They rebuilt the ummah's institutions and morale first. This is the Qur'anic principle of 13:11 in action: change the people within, and Allah changes their condition.

**The end of the Fatimid Caliphate:** Salahuddin's restoration of Egypt to Sunni rule (formally ending the Fatimid caliphate around 567 AH / 1171 CE) was a major turning point. We note, with adab, that this was a *political and sectarian* transition of its time; Salahuddin is generally credited with carrying it out with notable restraint. We narrate it factually and **without insulting** the Fatimids or Shia Muslims.

**Who was Salahuddin?** A Sunni Muslim of **Kurdish** origin — a reminder that the heroes of Islam come from every people. He was, by the testimony of friend and foe alike, personally humble, generous to the point of dying with almost no money, devoted to prayer, and famously merciful. His character (Episode 85) is as much his legacy as his victories.

**Sunni–Shia note (respectful):** This episode involves the Sunni reunification of a region that included the Shia Fatimid state. We present it within the series' Sunni framing, acknowledge the sectarian dimension honestly, and direct **no insult** at any group.

## Weak & Disputed Narrations

- ⚠️ Pious legends accumulated around Nur ad-Din and Salahuddin (miraculous dreams commanding the reconquest, prophetic visions, etc.) are largely **later embellishments**; their documented piety, justice, and strategy are impressive enough without them.

- ⚠️ The famous story of Nur ad-Din's *minbar*, built in advance for a liberated al-Aqsa, is well-attested in tradition; some narrative details are uncertain, but the minbar itself was real and *was* later placed in al-Aqsa by Salahuddin. ✓/⚠️

- ⚠️ Accounts of the precise manner of the Fatimid transition vary; that it was relatively bloodless is the general assessment, but details differ between sources.

## What We Don't Know

- ❓ The full extent of young Salahuddin's reluctance and early character (the sources are partly retrospective).
- ❓ Exact details and chronology of the Egyptian campaigns.
- ❓ How much of the unification was deliberate grand strategy versus opportunity seized — historians debate the balance.

## The Lessons

1. **Big victories rest on long, quiet groundwork.** Jerusalem was not retaken by one heroic charge but by decades of uniting, reforming, and institution-building. Honour the unglamorous work that makes the famous moment possible.
2. **Fix the inside first.** Nur ad-Din knew the war was won in the ummah's heart before it was won on the field. "*Allah will not change a people until they change themselves*" (13:11). Personal and communal revival precede external success.
3. **Unity is a precondition for great goals.** Egypt + Syria, once joined, changed everything. Some objectives are simply impossible while we remain divided — so the first battle is always the battle for unity.
4. **Allah raises leaders from unexpected places.** A reluctant young Kurd, not seeking power, became the liberator of al-Aqsa. Your background does not limit what Allah can do through a sincere heart.

**→ Next:** At a place called Hattin, beneath a burning sun, Salahuddin gambles everything — and within months he stands before the gates of Jerusalem, holding in his hands a choice that will define him forever.

# EPISODE 85 — THE MERCY OF SALAHUDDIN

## The Story

By **583 AH / 1187 CE**, Salahuddin had united Egypt and Syria and built a formidable army. The Crusader Kingdom of Jerusalem, meanwhile, was weakened by internal feuds and provoked by reckless lords — one of whom, Raynald of Châtillon, repeatedly broke truces and attacked Muslim pilgrim caravans, even threatening the holy cities of Makkah and Madinah. The time had come.

Salahuddin drew the Crusader army out into open battle on the parched hills of **Hattin**, near the Sea of Galilee, in the blazing heat of July **1187 CE**. He used the land itself as a weapon — cutting the heavily-armoured Crusaders off from water, surrounding them, setting the dry brush alight so smoke and thirst tormented them. The **Battle of Hattin** was a catastrophe for the Crusaders: their army was annihilated, their king captured, and the relic they carried as their holiest standard, the "True Cross," taken. In a single day, the military power of the Crusader kingdom was broken.

With the Crusader army destroyed, the cities of the Holy Land fell to Salahuddin one after another — and within months, he stood before **Jerusalem** itself. After a short siege, the city surrendered in **Rajab 583 AH / October 1187 CE.** Al-Aqsa was back in Muslim hands after eighty-eight years.

And now came the moment that defined Salahuddin for all time — the moment history holds up against the massacre of 1099.

Eighty-eight years earlier, the Crusaders had taken Jerusalem and **drowned it in blood.** Salahuddin took the same city — and chose **mercy.** There was no massacre. He guaranteed the safety of the inhabitants. He allowed the Christians of the city to ransom themselves for modest sums, and — famously — when many could not pay, he and his commanders let great numbers go **free anyway**, his own brother al-Adil reportedly requesting and freeing thousands, Salahuddin himself releasing the elderly, the poor, and freeing captives. He protected the Christian holy sites and the lives of civilians. The conquered were not slaughtered; they were escorted to safety. He even permitted Christian pilgrimage to continue.

This conduct so astonished medieval Europe that Salahuddin became a figure of **chivalric legend in the very lands that had marched against him** — celebrated in European poetry and story as the model of the noble, merciful knight. His mercy was not naivety or weakness; it flowed directly from the teachings of his faith: the Prophet's ﷺ prohibition of killing non-combatants, the command to honour treaties, the call to mercy.

The fall of Jerusalem triggered the **Third Crusade (1189–1192 CE)**, which brought the famous European king **Richard the Lionheart** of England to the Holy Land. The duel between Salahuddin and Richard became legendary — two great commanders locked in a hard-fought struggle along the coast. Richard won battles and retook some coastal cities; Salahuddin held the interior and, above all, **kept Jerusalem.** Their mutual respect became its own legend: when Richard fell ill, Salahuddin is said to have sent him fruit and snow from the mountains to cool his fever, and when Richard's horse was killed in battle, to have sent him fresh mounts — courtesies between enemies that the world never forgot. In the end they made a **truce (1192 CE):** the Crusaders kept a strip of the coast, Jerusalem remained Muslim, and Christian pilgrims were guaranteed safe access to their holy places.

Salahuddin died soon after, in **589 AH / 1193 CE**, in Damascus. The man who had ruled Egypt, Syria, and the Holy Land, who had commanded vast armies and treasuries, died with so little personal wealth that, the accounts say, **there was barely enough to pay for his own grave.** He had given it all away. He was buried in Damascus, near the great Umayyad Mosque, where his modest tomb still stands.

He had recovered the city of the Night Journey — and he had shown the world that the strength of a believer is measured not only by the battles he wins, but by the mercy he shows when he has the power to be cruel.

## The Qur'an Says

- **Surah Al-Mumtahanah (60:8)** — "*Allah does not forbid you from those who do not fight you because of religion and do not expel you from your homes — from being righteous toward them and acting justly toward them. Indeed, Allah loves those who act justly.*" — the Qur'anic root of Salahuddin's treatment of Jerusalem's Christians. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah An-Nahl (16:126)** — "*And if you punish [an enemy], punish with an equivalent of that with which you were harmed. But if you are patient — it is better for those who are patient.*" — Salahuddin held the power to repay 1099 in kind, and chose the better, patient way. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Fussilat (41:34)** — "*And not equal are the good deed and the bad deed. Repel [evil] by that which is better; and thereupon the one whom between you and him is enmity will become as though he was a devoted friend.*" — mercy that turned enemies into admirers. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Al-A'raf (7:199)** — "*Take what is easy, enjoin what is good, and turn away from the ignorant.*" — the prophetic disposition of magnanimity Salahuddin embodied. ✓ Qur'an.

## The Authentic Hadith

- **Mercy is the way to Allah's mercy:** *"Those who show mercy will be shown mercy by the Most Merciful. Be merciful to those on the earth, and the One above the heavens will be merciful to you."* — **Sunan at-Tirmidhi / Abu Dawud**, **sahih.** ✓ The hadith that explains Salahuddin's choice at Jerusalem.

- **Prohibition of killing non-combatants:** The Prophet ﷺ forbade the killing of women and children in war. — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ The rule that distinguished Salahuddin's conquest from the massacre that preceded it.

- **Honouring covenants:** *"Whoever kills a person under a covenant of protection will not smell the fragrance of Paradise..."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari.** ✓ The basis of his guarantees of safety.

- **Strength is self-control:** *"The strong person is not the one who overpowers others; the strong person is the one who controls himself at the moment of anger."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ The truest measure of Salahuddin's greatness — mastery of himself when he held the power to take revenge.

## Historical / Theological Context

**The contrast that defines the season:** 1099 (Crusader massacre) versus 1187 (Salahuddin's mercy) is one of history's most striking moral comparisons — and crucially, it is **not** Muslim propaganda; it is acknowledged by Western historians on the basis of European and Muslim sources alike. Salahuddin's magnanimity entered European legend *because Europeans themselves recorded and admired it.* This is the historical proof that the mercy taught by Islam is not a modern apologetic but a documented practice.

**Not a story against Christians:** The point of this contrast is **not** "Muslims good, Christians bad." Salahuddin fought specific medieval armies, made truces with them, respected individual enemies like Richard, and protected Christian civilians and pilgrims. The lesson is about the *standard of conduct the deen demands*, especially in victory — a standard binding on Muslims regardless of how others behave. Medieval Christians of that era are not the Christians of today, and the Qur'an's command to justice and kindness toward peaceful people (60:8) governs how Muslims are to live with their neighbours now.

**Salahuddin's personal character:** The sources — including his close companions Baha ad-Din ibn Shaddad and the historian Ibn al-Athir — describe a man of deep humility, constant prayer, overflowing generosity (he died nearly penniless from giving), patience under provocation, and a softness of heart (he was known to weep). His greatness was as much moral as military.

**Sunni–Shia note (respectful):** Salahuddin is honoured broadly across the Muslim world as a hero of the ummah. We present him within the series' Sunni framing and **insult no one.**

## Weak & Disputed Narrations

- ⚠️ The most beloved Saladin–Richard anecdotes (sending fruit and snow for Richard's fever, gifting him horses in battle) come from **medieval chronicle traditions** that mix fact and chivalric legend; they reflect a *real* mutual respect, but specific details are not all firmly documented. Tell them as celebrated tradition.

- ⚠️ Numbers (army sizes at Hattin, ransom figures and how many were freed at Jerusalem) vary across sources; the *pattern* — annihilation at Hattin, mercy at Jerusalem — is solid, the exact figures are not.

- ✗ Hateful retellings (in any direction) that erase Salahuddin's mercy, or that use Hattin to glorify cruelty, distort the documented history and contradict the very faith Salahuddin practised.

## What We Don't Know

- ❓ Exact casualty and ransom numbers.
- ❓ Which specific Saladin–Richard courtesies are literal history versus enhanced legend.
- ❓ Precise details of Salahuddin's final illness and last days.

## The Lessons

1. **Mercy in the hour of power is the summit of strength.** Salahuddin could have answered 1099 with 1099. He answered it with safety, ransom, and release. "*The strong one controls himself at the moment of anger.*" That choice, not the battle, is why the world still honours him.
2. **Let your conduct be governed by your faith, not by your enemy's.** He did not lower himself to the standard of those who had massacred; he held to the standard of the Prophet ﷺ. Principle does not flex to match provocation.
3. **Mercy can conquer what swords cannot.** Salahuddin won the lasting admiration of the very civilisation that came to destroy him — by being merciful. "*Repel evil with what is better, and your enemy becomes a devoted friend*" (41:34).
4. **Die empty-handed of the world, full-handed with Allah.** He ruled an empire and left barely enough for his grave, because he had sent his wealth ahead of him. Real riches are what you give away for Allah's sake.
