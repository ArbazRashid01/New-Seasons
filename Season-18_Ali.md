# SEASON 18 — ALI IBN ABI TALIB (R.A.)
### *The Lion of God and the Door of Knowledge — the cousin raised by the Prophet ﷺ, who inherited a fractured ummah and an unhealed wound.*

**Arc IX — The Rightly-Guided Era · Caliph 35–40 AH / 656–661 CE**

**Grading legend:** ✓ Qur'an / Sahih (authentic) · ⚠️ Weak or disputed · ✗ Isra'iliyyat or fabricated · ❓ genuinely unknown

> ⚠️ **MAXIMUM ADAB — read this first.** This season covers the most painful and most contested chapter in early Islamic history: the caliphate of Ali ibn Abi Talib (R.A.), the wars between Companions (the Camel and Siffin), the rise of the first extremists, and Ali's martyrdom. Here we follow a sacred discipline that the greatest early scholars adopted: **we narrate the established facts and we withhold judgement.** The Companions who differed and even fought were sincere believers exercising *ijtihad* (sincere reasoned effort); some were right and some erred, but **none are to be cursed, vilified, or made into villains.** Loving the Ahl al-Bayt (the Prophet's ﷺ family) and loving all the Companions are **not opposites** — the believer holds both. Sunni–Shia differences are flagged respectfully throughout; the Sunni position is presented, the Shia view is acknowledged to exist, and **no group or person is ever insulted.** This arc absolutely requires **Tier-A scholar review** before any teaching or publication.

# EPISODE 65 — THE CALIPH AMID THE STORM

## The Story

To understand the weight of what Ali (R.A.) now carried, you have to remember who he was — because few human beings have ever been so close to the Prophet ﷺ.

Ali ibn Abi Talib was the Prophet's ﷺ **first cousin**, raised in his household as a boy. He was, by the most reliable accounts, **the first child to accept Islam.** He was the youth who slept in the Prophet's ﷺ bed on the night of the Hijrah, offering his own life as a decoy so the Messenger ﷺ could escape Makkah. He married **Fatimah (R.A.)**, the Prophet's ﷺ beloved daughter, and through them came **Hasan and Husayn (R.A.)**, the Prophet's ﷺ grandsons whom he cherished. Ali was the warrior whose courage at Badr, Uhud, the Trench, and Khaybar became legend — the "Lion of God." And he was a fountain of knowledge; the Companions turned to him for the deepest questions of the deen.

When Uthman (R.A.) was murdered and the ummah stood leaderless and stunned, the people of Madinah turned to Ali. He was, by the reckoning of the overwhelming majority, the best man alive for the trust. He initially **refused** — he could see the storm coming, and he did not want power born from chaos. But the people pressed him until he accepted the pledge (*bay'ah*) in **35 AH / 656 CE**, becoming the fourth Rightly-Guided Caliph.

He inherited an impossible situation. The murder of Uthman had left a gaping wound: the killers were a mixed mob who had melted back into the factions and could not be cleanly identified. A powerful cry rose across the ummah — **"qisas!"** — justice, retribution for the blood of the murdered caliph. But Ali faced a brutal practical reality: with the assassins unidentified and entangled among armed factions, immediate retribution risked igniting a wider civil war that could consume everything. His judgement (*ijtihad*) was to first **stabilise** the state, establish his authority, and restore order — *then* pursue justice for Uthman's blood through proper process.

Others, equally sincere, made a different judgement: that justice for a murdered caliph could not wait, that order itself depended on it. **This honest difference of ijtihad — not malice on anyone's part — is the seed of the tragedies that follow.** Both positions came from a love of justice and of Islam. That is the heartbreak of this era: it was not a war of good against evil, but of good people who read an impossible situation differently.

Ali ruled from Kufa, lived in legendary simplicity, and embodied the justice and knowledge the Prophet ﷺ had praised in him. But the storm would not let him govern in peace.

## The Qur'an Says

- **Surah Al-Ma'idah (5:55)** — "*Your ally is none but Allah and His Messenger and those who have believed — those who establish prayer and give zakah, and they bow [in worship].*" — Sunni tafsir affirms its general meaning of believers' allegiance to Allah and His Messenger; some narrations connect its revelation to an act of charity by Ali (R.A.). (Shia tradition reads it as a specific designation of Ali's authority — *wilayah*.) We note the Sunni reading and the Shia reading respectfully. ✓ Qur'an / ⚠️ on specific application.

- **Surah Al-Ahzab (33:33)** — the **Verse of Purification (Ayat at-Tatheer):** "*...Allah only intends to remove from you the impurity, O People of the Household (Ahl al-Bayt), and to purify you with [extensive] purification.*" — In authentic narrations the Prophet ﷺ gathered Ali, Fatimah, Hasan, and Husayn under his cloak and included them in this honour (the *Hadith al-Kisa* — Sahih Muslim). The love and honour of the Ahl al-Bayt is rooted here. ✓ Qur'an + Sahih.

- **Surah Aal-e-Imran (3:103)** — "*And hold firmly to the rope of Allah all together and do not become divided...*" — the very command the ummah was about to be tested against. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Al-Hujurat (49:9–10)** — "*And if two factions among the believers should fight, then make settlement between the two... The believers are but brothers, so make settlement between your brothers...*" — astonishingly, the Qur'an itself anticipates *believers* fighting believers, calls both sides "believers" and "brothers," and commands reconciliation. This ayah is the lens through which Sunni scholars view the Camel and Siffin. ✓ Qur'an.

## The Authentic Hadith

- **"As Harun was to Musa":** When the Prophet ﷺ left Ali in charge of Madinah during the Tabuk expedition, Ali felt grief at being left behind, and the Prophet ﷺ said, *"Are you not pleased that you are to me as Harun (Aaron) was to Musa (Moses) — except that there is no prophet after me?"* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim**. ✓ A statement of deep love and trust (the explicit exception "no prophet after me" is part of the hadith itself).

- **The banner of Khaybar:** At Khaybar the Prophet ﷺ said he would give the banner to *"a man who loves Allah and His Messenger, and whom Allah and His Messenger love"* — and he gave it to Ali, through whose hand Allah granted victory. — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim**. ✓

- **Love of Ali is a sign of faith:** *"None loves Ali but a believer, and none hates him but a hypocrite."* — **Sahih Muslim**. ✓

- **"The Door of Knowledge":** The widely-quoted *"I am the city of knowledge and Ali is its gate"* is, by the verdict of hadith critics, **weak (da'if)** in its chains (⚠️). However, Ali's profound knowledge is firmly established by many authentic reports and by the consensus of the Companions, who deferred to him on the hardest questions. So the *title* is weak; the *reality* of his deep knowledge is sound. ⚠️ on the specific hadith, ✓ on the fact.

- **Ghadir Khumm — "whoever's mawla I am, Ali is his mawla":** The Prophet ﷺ said, *"Whoever I am his mawla, then Ali is his mawla."* — this is reported through **authentic chains** (graded sahih by many scholars, including in Tirmidhi and Musnad Ahmad). ✓ **(See the context section for the crucial Sunni–Shia difference over its meaning.)**

## Historical / Theological Context

**The Saqifah and the question of succession (flashback context):** When the Prophet ﷺ passed away (11 AH), the Ansar gathered at the *Saqifah* (the covered courtyard) of Banu Sa'idah to discuss leadership, and the Muhajirun joined; the gathering pledged to **Abu Bakr (R.A.)**. Ali (R.A.) pledged to Abu Bakr (the well-known reports place his full, public pledge after the passing of Fatimah, R.A., some months later — a delay Sunni scholars attribute to the circumstances of grief and process, not to rejection of Abu Bakr's legitimacy).
- **Sunni position:** leadership was *not* divinely designated to a specific person; the community rightly chose Abu Bakr, then Umar, then Uthman, then Ali — all four are the Rightly-Guided Caliphs in order of merit and tenure.
- **Shia position:** the Prophet ﷺ explicitly designated Ali as his immediate successor (notably citing **Ghadir Khumm**), so the first three caliphates are seen as a departure from that designation.
- We present the Sunni reading, acknowledge the Shia reading with full respect, and **insult no one.**

**Ghadir Khumm — the meaning, respectfully:** The *event* and the words ("whoever's mawla I am, Ali is his mawla") are authentic. The **difference is over meaning:**
- **Sunni understanding:** *mawla* here means beloved friend/protector/ally; the Prophet ﷺ was affirming Ali's high standing and defending him against some who had criticised his conduct on an expedition — a statement of love and loyalty, not an appointment to rule.
- **Shia understanding:** *mawla* here means master/leader (*wali al-amr*), constituting an explicit designation of Ali's succession (*wilayah*/imamah).
- Same authentic text; two sincere readings. ✓ on the hadith; the interpretation is the point of difference.

**Fadak (respectful note):** *Fadak* was a piece of land; after the Prophet's ﷺ death, Fatimah (R.A.) requested it as inheritance, and Abu Bakr (R.A.) declined, citing the Prophet's ﷺ statement *"We [Prophets] are not inherited; what we leave is charity"* (**Sahih al-Bukhari**). Sunni scholars view this as Abu Bakr applying a prophetic ruling, with no malice toward Fatimah (R.A.), whom he loved. Shia tradition views the matter differently and as a grievance. We note the authentic hadith and the existence of the difference, **without impugning anyone's character.**

**The impossible task:** The defining political reality of Ali's caliphate is the tension between **immediate retribution for Uthman's blood** and **stabilising the state first.** Both were legitimate concerns. Ali's *ijtihad* favoured stabilisation; others' *ijtihad* favoured immediate justice. Hold this firmly: it frames everything in Episode 66.

## Weak & Disputed Narrations

- ⚠️ **"I am the city of knowledge and Ali is its gate"** — weak in chain (see above), though Ali's knowledge is authentically established by other means.

- ⚠️ A large body of *fada'il* (virtue) narrations about Ali (R.A.) circulate in both exaggerated pro-Ali and hostile anti-Ali forms; **both extremes were fabricated** by partisans during later conflicts. Hadith critics (e.g., in the works on *mawdu'at*, fabricated reports) catalogued many. Rely only on what is graded sahih/hasan.

- ✗ Any narration *cursing* or demeaning Ali (R.A.) is fabricated and rejected — to hate him is, by Sahih Muslim, a sign of hypocrisy.
- ✗ Any narration *deifying* Ali or raising him above the station of a human Companion is likewise fabricated and rejected (the extremist *ghulat* sects were condemned by Ali himself).

## What We Don't Know

- ❓ The exact timing and circumstances of Ali's pledge to Abu Bakr are reported with some variation; the fact of the pledge is established.
- ❓ The precise internal calculations behind Ali's decision to prioritise stabilisation over immediate retribution — we know the broad reasoning, not every detail.
- ❓ Much of the fine detail of this period is buried under partisan reporting; certainty is limited to the broad, well-attested outline.

## The Lessons

1. **Sometimes the right leader inherits an impossible situation.** Ali (R.A.) did nothing to create the crisis; he accepted the burden of healing it. Leadership is often about carrying problems you did not make.
2. **Sincere people can disagree profoundly — and both can be seeking good.** The fault-line of this era was *ijtihad*, not evil. Train yourself to imagine the sincerity of those you disagree with.
3. **Love the Prophet's ﷺ family.** The Qur'an purified the Ahl al-Bayt (33:33) and the Prophet ﷺ loved them dearly. Honouring them is not optional, and it does not compete with honouring the other Companions.
4. **Guard against both extremes.** Islam rejected those who hated Ali *and* those who deified him. The straight path runs between vilification and exaggeration.

**→ Next:** The cry for justice and the call for stability collide on the battlefield — and for the first time, the swords of the Companions are drawn against one another. We will narrate, and we will not judge.

# EPISODE 66 — BROTHERS AT WAR

## The Story

This is the episode that the early scholars approached on their knees. What happened here is real, painful, and it must be told — but told the way the great imams told it: **with our hands held back from judgement.**

### The Battle of the Camel (Jamal) — 36 AH

A group of senior, sincere Companions — including **Aisha (R.A.)**, the Mother of the Believers and the Prophet's ﷺ beloved wife, along with **Talha ibn Ubaydullah** and **Az-Zubayr ibn al-Awwam (R.A.)**, both among the ten promised Paradise — were anguished by the unpunished murder of Uthman. They were *not* rebelling against Ali's right to lead; they marched toward Basra demanding swift justice (*qisas*) for Uthman's blood and an end to the lawlessness, fearing that delay would let the murderers escape and embolden chaos.

Ali (R.A.) shared their grief and their desire for justice — but held that the state must first be stabilised before retribution could be safely and rightly pursued. Negotiations actually moved toward **reconciliation.** By the soundest understanding, the two camps were close to agreement.

And then — catastrophe. By the accounts most trusted by Sunni scholars, **the agitators who had murdered Uthman, terrified that a reconciliation would expose and condemn them, attacked under cover of darkness to ignite the fighting** they needed to survive. Each side, in the confusion, believed the other had betrayed the truce. Battle erupted around the camel that carried Aisha's (R.A.) howdah — giving the day its name.

It was a tragedy. **Talha and Az-Zubayr (R.A.) were both killed** — Zubayr, by the famous report, had actually *withdrawn* from the battle after Ali reminded him of a word of the Prophet ﷺ, and was killed treacherously while leaving. When the fighting ended, **Ali (R.A.) treated Aisha (R.A.) with the utmost honour and reverence**, wept, and personally arranged for her safe and dignified return to Madinah with an escort. There was no vengeance, no humiliation — there was grief between people who loved the same Prophet ﷺ.

### The Battle of Siffin — 37 AH

The second confrontation was with **Mu'awiyah ibn Abi Sufyan (R.A.)**, the governor of Syria and a kinsman of Uthman. Mu'awiyah refused to give the pledge (*bay'ah*) to Ali until Uthman's killers were handed over for justice. Ali held that, as caliph, allegiance came first and justice would follow through due process, and that he could not simply surrender men amid the chaos. **Both invoked the blood of Uthman; they differed on the sequence — pledge-then-justice versus justice-then-pledge.** Again: a difference of *ijtihad*, not a clash of belief.

The armies met at **Siffin**, on the Euphrates, in 37 AH. The fighting was long and terrible — believers on both sides who prayed toward the same qiblah. As Ali's side gained the upper hand, men in Mu'awiyah's army **raised pages of the Qur'an (masahif) on their spears**, calling for the matter to be settled by the Book of Allah rather than the sword. The fighting halted, and both sides agreed to **arbitration (tahkim)** — to appoint representatives to seek a resolution by the Qur'an.

The arbitration did not resolve the conflict cleanly, and its ambiguous outcome would split Ali's own camp — sowing the seed of the extremists we meet in the next episode. But the swords, for now, were sheathed.

### How we hold this

The Sunni discipline here is firm and ancient: **we do not take sides, and we do not curse.** Ali (R.A.) is regarded by the majority as having been **closer to the truth** in these disputes (the Prophet ﷺ had foretold his rightness in certain matters), and his caliphate was the legitimate one. But Talha, Zubayr, Aisha, and Mu'awiyah (R.A.) were **sincere Companions**, not villains — people who erred in *ijtihad* are not sinners but rewarded for their sincerity even when mistaken, as the Prophet ﷺ taught about the sincere judge who errs. We say of all of them: *radiyallahu anhum* — may Allah be pleased with them — and we leave the final accounting to Allah, exactly as the early scholars did.

## The Qur'an Says

- **Surah Al-Hujurat (49:9–10)** — "*And if two factions among the believers should fight, then make settlement between the two... The believers are but brothers...*" — the foundational ayah: even believers who fight remain *believers* and *brothers*; the command is reconciliation, not excommunication. This is exactly how Sunni scholars frame the Camel and Siffin. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Aal-e-Imran (3:103)** — "*...and do not become divided.*" — the warning whose violation broke the ummah's heart. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah An-Nisa (4:59)** — "*O you who have believed, obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you. And if you disagree over anything, refer it to Allah and His Messenger...*" — the principle of referring disputes back to the Qur'an, which is literally what the raising of the masahif at Siffin appealed to. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Al-Hashr (59:10)** — "*...Our Lord, forgive us and our brothers who preceded us in faith and put not in our hearts [any] resentment toward those who have believed...*" — the du'a that defines the believer's posture toward the Companions: ask forgiveness for them, harbour no rancour. ✓ Qur'an.

## The Authentic Hadith

- **The Ammar hadith (handle with balance):** The Prophet ﷺ said of **Ammar ibn Yasir (R.A.)**, *"Woe to Ammar — a transgressing party (al-fi'ah al-baghiyah) will kill him."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim**. ✓ Ammar (R.A.) was killed at Siffin fighting **on Ali's side.** Sunni scholars therefore take this as an authentic indication that **Ali's side was closer to the truth** and the opposing party was, in the technical sense, the "transgressing" (mistaken) one. **But note the adab carefully:** the word *baghiyah* (transgressing) here denotes a party that *erred in ijtihad* — it does **not** make them disbelievers, and it does **not** license cursing them. The early scholars used this hadith to identify the more-correct side **without** condemning the persons of the other Companions. We state it plainly and stop exactly there.

- **Zubayr's withdrawal:** It is authentically reported that the Prophet ﷺ had foretold that Ali and Zubayr would one day fight, and that Zubayr would be in the wrong in that instance — and when Ali reminded Zubayr of a relevant word of the Prophet ﷺ at the Camel, Zubayr withdrew from the battle. (Reported in reliable collections; reflects the sincerity and God-fearing nature of these men — a reminder withdrew a Companion from war.) ✓/⚠️ (core is sound; some versions vary).

- **The Prophet's ﷺ love for Aisha (R.A.):** Her unmatched standing as a Mother of the Believers and a great scholar of the ummah is established across **Bukhari and Muslim.** Whatever her *ijtihad* at the Camel, her honour is inviolable. ✓

- **Foretelling reconciliation through Hasan:** The Prophet ﷺ said of his grandson Hasan, *"This son of mine is a sayyid (leader), and perhaps Allah will reconcile two great factions of the Muslims through him."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari**. ✓ (A prophecy fulfilled in the next season — pointing forward to peace.)

## Historical / Theological Context

**The golden rule of this era (Sunni creed):** It is a settled principle of *Ahl as-Sunnah wal-Jama'ah* that we **restrain our tongues** regarding the disputes among the Companions (*nimsiku 'amma shajara bayna as-sahabah*). We assume the best, recognise their sincerity, acknowledge that some were nearer the truth than others, and refer the rest to Allah. This is not evasion — it is *adab* (reverence) commanded by the love and gratitude we owe the generation that carried Islam to us.

**Were these "civil wars" a stain on them?** No — they are a stain on the *fitnah*, not on the persons. The Qur'an (49:9–10) already calls fighting believers "believers and brothers." Erring in sincere *ijtihad* earns one reward (the Prophet ﷺ: the judge who strives and errs gets one reward, and the one who strives and is correct gets two — Bukhari/Muslim). So even the "mistaken" Companions are, by the Prophet's ﷺ own teaching, **rewarded for their sincerity.** This is why we never speak ill of them.

**Sunni–Shia note (respectful):** Sunni and Shia traditions narrate these wars very differently and reach different conclusions about culpability, especially regarding Mu'awiyah, Aisha, Talha, and Zubayr (R.A.). The Sunni approach — honour all, judge none, hold Ali as the legitimate caliph nearer the truth — is presented here. The Shia tradition is more critical of Ali's opponents. We acknowledge the difference, present the Sunni position, and **direct no curse or insult at any individual or group.** This is non-negotiable adab.

## Weak & Disputed Narrations

- ⚠️ **Most of the detailed battle narratives, casualty figures, speeches, and especially the assignment of blame** for the Camel and Siffin come through **partisan, often weak or fabricated chains** (heavily via at-Tabari's collected reports and later sources). The *outlines* above are the well-attested core; the lurid details and the villain-narratives are not reliable.

- ⚠️ Reports claiming Aisha, Talha, or Zubayr (R.A.) sought *power* (rather than justice for Uthman) are disputed and contradicted by stronger reports; the established motive is the demand for *qisas*.

- ✗ All narrations that **curse** Mu'awiyah, Aisha, or any Companion — or that strip them of faith — are **rejected** as either fabricated or as the sinful innovation of sectarian extremism. Cursing Companions is condemned in Sunni Islam.

- ⚠️ The claim that the arbitration's representatives were "tricked" in a specific dramatic scene is a popular but **weakly-sourced** dramatization; the *outcome* (inconclusive, divisive) is what's certain.

## What We Don't Know

- ❓ The exact spark of the Camel — the "night attack by the agitators" is the account Sunni scholars favour, but absolute certainty about who struck first is beyond us.
- ❓ True casualty numbers (the sources exaggerate).
- ❓ The precise terms and proceedings of the Siffin arbitration.
- ❓ The inner intentions of each Companion — known only to Allah, which is *precisely why* we withhold judgement.

## The Lessons

1. **Guard your tongue about people greater than you.** If the Companions who carried Islam to us erred in *ijtihad* yet are still honoured and rewarded, who are we to curse them? Restraint here is a test of our own faith.
2. **Fitnah weaponises sincerity.** Good people, both wanting justice, were maneuvered into war by those who feared justice. Beware how easily righteous anger is steered toward destruction.
3. **A single reminder can stop a war.** Zubayr (R.A.) walked away from the battlefield when reminded of the Prophet's ﷺ word. The God-fearing heart is reachable — never underestimate a sincere reminder.
4. **Hold the rope of Allah together (3:103).** The deepest lesson of this episode is the catastrophic cost of division — even *sincere* division. Unity is not a luxury; it is a command.

**→ Next:** From the ashes of the arbitration rises a new and terrifying force — men who declare everyone but themselves disbelievers, and turn their swords on the very best of the ummah. The first extremists have arrived.

# EPISODE 67 — THE FIRST EXTREMISTS & A MARTYR AT DAWN

## The Story

When Ali (R.A.) agreed to arbitration at Siffin, a faction within his *own* army turned on him in fury. Their slogan was seductive and deadly: **"La hukma illa lillah"** — "Judgement belongs to Allah alone." To them, agreeing to human arbitration was a betrayal: how dare men arbitrate what (they claimed) only Allah could decide? Ali answered them with one of history's wisest lines — that their slogan was *"a word of truth by which falsehood is intended,"* because the Qur'an *itself* commands arbitration in disputes (he cited Allah's own appointment of arbiters in the Qur'an). But they would not listen.

These were the **Khawarij (Kharijites)** — the "Seceders" — and they are the **first extremist sect in Islamic history.** They broke away, gathered at a place called **Harura** and then **Nahrawan**, and developed a monstrous doctrine: they made **takfir** (declared as disbelievers) any Muslim who disagreed with them — including Ali, the cousin of the Prophet ﷺ, and the great Companions — and they considered the blood of such "disbelievers" lawful to spill. They were, by all reports, intensely "religious" on the surface: they prayed long, fasted, recited the Qur'an constantly, and were utterly sincere in their delusion. **That is the chilling lesson of the Khawarij: extremism wears the mask of piety.**

The Prophet ﷺ had **warned of them by description, decades earlier.** He described people who would recite the Qur'an but it would not pass their throats (it would not reach their hearts), who would "shoot out of the religion as an arrow shoots out of the game," who would be young in age and foolish in understanding, killing the people of Islam while leaving the idol-worshippers alone. He even commanded that whoever lived to see them should fight them, for there is reward in opposing them. This is among the most authentic of prophecies — in **Bukhari and Muslim.**

The Khawarij turned to terror, murdering Muslims — including, infamously, the Companion **Abdullah ibn Khabbab (R.A.)** and his pregnant wife, in cold blood. Ali (R.A.) confronted them at the **Battle of Nahrawan (38 AH)** and defeated them decisively. But the ideology did not die with them; it would resurface again and again across the centuries, and it survives in the violent extremists of our own time who, exactly as the Prophet ﷺ described, drape murder in the language of faith.

### The Martyrdom of Ali (R.A.)

The Khawarij did not forgive Nahrawan. A small cell of them plotted to assassinate, on a single night, the three men they blamed for the ummah's state — Ali, Mu'awiyah, and Amr ibn al-As (R.A.). The plots against Mu'awiyah and Amr failed. The plot against Ali did not.

In **Ramadan, 40 AH / 661 CE**, as Ali ibn Abi Talib (R.A.) went out to lead the **Fajr prayer** in the mosque of Kufa — calling the people to the prayer he loved — the Kharijite **Abd al-Rahman ibn Muljam** struck him with a poisoned sword. The Lion of God, the cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet ﷺ, the father of Hasan and Husayn, the fourth Rightly-Guided Caliph, was mortally wounded **at the prayer**, just as Umar (R.A.) had been before him.

In his final days he was a model of justice even toward his own killer: he instructed that Ibn Muljam be treated humanely while alive, and that if he himself died, the man be given a single just execution in retribution and not be mutilated — and he reminded his sons to fear Allah. He passed from this world a martyr.

With Ali's death, the era of the **Khulafa ar-Rashidun — the Rightly-Guided Caliphs — came to its close**, just as the Prophet ﷺ had indicated: *"The caliphate [upon the prophetic methodology] will last thirty years."* (Sunan at-Tirmidhi, sahih) — and from the Prophet's ﷺ death to the end of Ali's caliphate, with the brief caliphate of his son Hasan to follow, was almost exactly that span.

## The Qur'an Says

- **Surah Al-Ma'idah (5:32)** — "*...whoever kills a soul unless for a soul or for corruption in the land — it is as if he had slain mankind entirely...*" — the sanctity of life the Khawarij trampled with their bloodshed. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah An-Nisa (4:93)** — "*But whoever kills a believer intentionally — his recompense is Hell, wherein he will abide eternally...*" — the terrible warning against killing a believer, which the extremists invited upon themselves by their takfir-and-murder. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Al-Kahf (18:103–104)** — "*Say, [O Muhammad], 'Shall we inform you of the greatest losers as to [their] deeds? [They are] those whose effort is lost in worldly life, while they think that they are doing well in work.'*" — the precise psychology of the Khawarij: intense religious effort, utterly misdirected, while certain of their own righteousness. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Al-An'am (6:159)** — "*Indeed, those who have divided their religion and become sects — you have no part with them in anything...*" — a warning against sectarian fragmentation. ✓ Qur'an.

## The Authentic Hadith

- **The prophecy of the Khawarij:** The Prophet ﷺ said there would arise people *"young in age, foolish in mind, who will speak the best words of creation (i.e., recite the Qur'an), but their faith will not pass beyond their throats. They will pass out of the religion as an arrow passes out of the game."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ He commanded that whoever meets them should fight them, for there is reward for that on the Day of Resurrection.

- **They kill the Muslims and leave the idolaters:** The Prophet ﷺ said this faction *"will kill the people of Islam and leave the people of idols."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ A precise diagnosis of extremist terror, then and now.

- **"The best of you will kill them" / Ali's role:** Ali (R.A.) himself recounted the Prophet's ﷺ description of the Khawarij and the sign among them (a man with a deformed arm), which was found among the slain at Nahrawan — confirming for him that fighting them was exactly what the Prophet ﷺ had foretold. — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓

- **The thirty-year caliphate:** *"The caliphate (of prophethood) will be thirty years; then Allah will give the kingdom (mulk) to whomever He wills."* — **Sunan at-Tirmidhi** and **Musnad Ahmad**, graded **sahih**. ✓ Marking the close of the Rashidun era.

## Historical / Theological Context

**Who were the Khawarij — and why they still matter:** They are the prototype of all violent religious extremism. Their defining features — pronouncing *takfir* on fellow Muslims over disagreements, declaring Muslim blood lawful, intense outward worship masking a hardened heart, youthful zeal without scholarly grounding, and rebellion against legitimate authority — recur in every era. Mainstream Sunni scholarship has *always* identified modern terrorist movements that excommunicate and murder Muslims as the spiritual descendants of the Khawarij. The Prophet's ﷺ warning is therefore not ancient history; it is a live diagnosis.

**The wisdom of Ali (R.A.) with them:** Notably, Ali did *not* declare the Khawarij to be disbelievers, even as he fought them — he treated them as Muslims who had rebelled and shed blood unlawfully. This nuance became foundational to Sunni political theology: even rebels are not lightly excommunicated.

**The close of the Rashidun:** With Ali's martyrdom in 40 AH, the model era of caliphate-on-the-prophetic-method ends (completed by the brief caliphate of Hasan, R.A. — Season 19). What follows is *mulk* (kingship/dynastic rule), beginning with the Umayyads — a real and consequential transition the Prophet ﷺ had signalled.

**Sunni–Shia note (respectful):** Both traditions revile the Khawarij and honour Ali (R.A.) as wrongfully martyred — here the traditions largely converge in grief. Sunnis count Ali as the fourth Rightly-Guided Caliph; Shia revere him as the first Imam and the rightful successor from the beginning. We present the Sunni framing and acknowledge the Shia reverence with full respect. **No insult to anyone.**

## Weak & Disputed Narrations

- ⚠️ Dramatic verbatim debates between Ali (R.A.) and the Khawarij (e.g., the famous Ibn Abbas dialogue at Harura) contain a **sound core** but circulate in embellished forms; the *substance* (their slogan refuted by the Qur'an's own command to arbitrate) is reliable.

- ⚠️ The exact details and dialogue of the assassination night, and Ibn Muljam's precise motive and fate, vary across sources; the core (Fajr, poisoned sword, Ramadan 40 AH, Ali's instruction of just and non-mutilating retribution) is well-attested.

- ✗ Any narration glorifying the Khawarij or Ibn Muljam, or justifying takfir-violence, is rejected as contrary to explicit Sahih hadith.

## What We Don't Know

- ❓ The full size of the Kharijite movement and exact Nahrawan casualties.
- ❓ The precise day of Ali's martyrdom within Ramadan 40 AH (reports differ on the date; commonly the 17th, 19th, or 21st).
- ❓ The complete details of the assassination plot and whether the cell was larger than the three named targets.

## The Lessons

1. **Extremism wears the mask of piety.** The Khawarij prayed, fasted, and recited more than most — and murdered the best of the ummah. Outward religiosity is not a substitute for knowledge, mercy, and obedience to legitimate scholarship and authority.
2. **Beware the slogan that is "truth used for falsehood."** "Judgement belongs to Allah alone" is *true* — yet they wielded it to justify rebellion and murder. Test slogans by the whole Qur'an and Sunnah, not a fragment.
3. **Takfir is a deadly door — keep it shut.** Ali (R.A.) fought rebels without expelling them from Islam. Casually declaring Muslims to be disbelievers is the Kharijite disease; the believer is slow to excommunicate and quick to excuse.
4. **The love of prayer marks the friends of Allah — even to the last breath.** Like Umar before him, Ali (R.A.) was struck down going *toward* the Fajr prayer. Their lives ended where their hearts already lived: in salah.
