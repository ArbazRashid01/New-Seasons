# EPISODE 74 — THE HOUSE OF MARWAN

## The Story

When the dust of Karbala settled in 61 AH, the Muslim world did not heal — it convulsed. The murder of the Prophet's ﷺ grandson sent a wave of grief and outrage across the ummah, and the next two decades were among the most turbulent in Islamic history. This is the period historians call the **Second Fitnah.**

Two great movements of conscience rose in the years after Husayn's (R.A.) martyrdom. In Kufa, a group called the **Tawwabun — "the Penitents"** — men who had failed to defend Husayn and were now consumed with remorse, marched out, essentially to die seeking forgiveness for their abandonment of him. And in the holy city of Makkah, one of the most respected men alive, **Abdullah ibn az-Zubayr (R.A.)** — son of the great Companion Zubayr and of Asma bint Abi Bakr, and grandson of As-Siddiq — refused to recognise the Umayyad house and was himself acknowledged as caliph across much of the Muslim world. For years, the ummah effectively had two centres: Ibn az-Zubayr in the Hijaz, and the Umayyads in Syria. We narrate this division as the early scholars did — as a tragedy among believers — without vilifying either side.

Into this chaos stepped a formidable figure: **Marwan ibn al-Hakam**, and after him his son, **Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan.** It is from Marwan that the ruling branch takes its name — the **Marwanids** — and it was Abd al-Malik who, through a long and often brutal struggle (the campaigns of his general al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf, a figure remembered for both his effectiveness and his harshness), finally reunited the empire under one authority by **73 AH / 692 CE**, after the fall of Makkah and the death of Ibn az-Zubayr (R.A.).

What Abd al-Malik did *next* shaped Islamic civilisation permanently. He was a builder of states. He **Arabised the administration** — for the first time, the government's record-keeping, which had still been run in Greek and Persian inherited from the old empires, was switched to **Arabic**, binding the diverse empire together through one language of governance. He **reformed the coinage**, minting the first distinctly Islamic gold *dinar* and silver *dirham*, stamped not with the faces of emperors but with the words of *tawhid* — the oneness of Allah. Money itself now proclaimed *La ilaha illa Allah.*

And he raised, on the most sacred rock in Jerusalem, the building that still crowns the skyline of the holy city: the **Dome of the Rock (Qubbat as-Sakhrah)**, completed around **72 AH / 691 CE** — the oldest surviving masterpiece of Islamic architecture, its inscriptions among the earliest monumental Qur'anic texts in the world. It stands beside Masjid al-Aqsa, on the blessed precinct the Qur'an calls "the farthest mosque."

From the ashes of fitnah, Abd al-Malik built the skeleton of an empire that would soon stretch farther than Rome ever did.

## The Qur'an Says

- **Surah Al-Isra (17:1)** — "*Exalted is He who took His Servant by night from al-Masjid al-Haram to al-Masjid al-Aqsa, whose surroundings We have blessed...*" — the Qur'anic sanctity of Jerusalem, the precinct upon which the Dome of the Rock was raised. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Al-Hajj (22:41)** — "*[They are] those who, if We give them authority in the land, establish prayer and give zakah and enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong...*" — the Qur'anic measure of those granted power, by which every dynasty is weighed. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah An-Nisa (4:59)** — "*O you who have believed, obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you...*" — the basis on which Sunni scholars counselled obedience to established Muslim authority for the sake of order, even while criticising specific wrongs. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Al-Hashr (59:10)** — "*...Our Lord, forgive us and our brothers who preceded us in faith, and put not in our hearts [any] resentment toward those who have believed...*" — the believer's posture toward the generations before us, including those who erred. ✓ Qur'an.

## The Authentic Hadith

- **Caliphate, then kingship:** *"The caliphate (upon the prophetic method) will be thirty years; then Allah will give the kingdom (mulk) to whomever He wills."* — **Sunan at-Tirmidhi** / **Musnad Ahmad**, graded **sahih.** ✓ The Umayyad state is precisely this transition from *khilafah* to *mulk* foretold by the Prophet ﷺ.

- **The stages of authority (cited with care):** A well-known narration of Hudhayfah (R.A.) describes prophethood, then caliphate on the prophetic method, then "biting kingship" (*mulkan 'addan*), then "tyrannical kingship" (*mulkan jabriyyan*), then a return to caliphate on the prophetic method. — **Musnad Ahmad.** ⚠️ Its grading is **disputed** (some scholars grade it hasan, others weaken it); we cite it as a *framework many scholars used*, not as a settled Sahih proof.

- **The virtue of Jerusalem (Bayt al-Maqdis):** The Prophet ﷺ said, *"Do not set out on a journey except to three mosques: al-Masjid al-Haram, this mosque of mine (in Madinah), and al-Masjid al-Aqsa."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ The standing of the place Abd al-Malik adorned.

- **Patience under flawed rulers:** The Prophet ﷺ foretold that after him would come rulers whose conduct the people would dislike, and counselled the believers to **give them their due and ask Allah for their own due** — i.e., to preserve unity and avoid bloodshed rather than rush to rebellion. — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ This shaped the mainstream Sunni response to imperfect Umayyad rule.

## Historical / Theological Context

**Why "Umayyad"?** The dynasty descends from **Umayyah**, a clan of Quraysh; the caliphs traced to Abu Sufyan (the Sufyanid line: Mu'awiyah, Yazid, and briefly Mu'awiyah II) and then to Marwan (the Marwanid line: Abd al-Malik and his successors). They ruled from **Damascus** in Syria — the first time the centre of the Muslim world moved out of Arabia.

**Kingship, honestly assessed:** The defining theological fact of this era is the shift from *consultative caliphate* to *hereditary monarchy.* Sunni scholarship neither sanctifies this nor treats the Umayyads as illegitimate rulers; the mainstream view, rooted in the hadiths above, was to recognise the established authority for the sake of unity and security, while freely identifying its failings. The Umayyads gave the ummah real goods (stability, expansion, institutions) and real harms (dynastic succession, harsh governors like al-Hajjaj, and grievances against the Ahl al-Bayt and others).

**The Dome of the Rock — what it is and isn't:** It is a shrine/monument over the rock on the Aqsa precinct, *not* the congregational Masjid al-Aqsa itself (the silver-grey-domed mosque nearby). Both sit within the blessed sanctuary (*al-Haram ash-Sharif*) the Qur'an honours. Its construction was a statement of Islamic confidence and a gift of beauty to the holy city.

**Sunni–Shia note (respectful):** This era is remembered very differently across traditions. For Shia Islam, the Umayyad period is bound up with the suffering of the Ahl al-Bayt (Karbala and after) and is viewed critically. Sunni Islam also mourns the wrongs done to the Prophet's ﷺ family and criticises the era's injustices, while still treating the Umayyad caliphs as Muslim rulers within the framework above. We present the Sunni framing, acknowledge the Shia perspective with respect, and **insult no one.** A crucial healing act by a later Umayyad — the ending of the public cursing of Ali (R.A.) — comes in Episode 76.

## Weak & Disputed Narrations

- ⚠️ The figure of **al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf** attracts both exaggerated demonisation and exaggerated defence; many lurid anecdotes about his cruelty come through unreliable chains. That he was an effective but harsh governor is established; the gory specifics often are not.

- ⚠️ Detailed accounts of the Tawwabun and of al-Mukhtar ath-Thaqafi's later movement in Kufa (which claimed to avenge Husayn) are heavily **partisan** and contested; al-Mukhtar in particular is judged very differently by different sources. Treat the dramatic detail with caution.

- ⚠️ The precise motives attributed to Abd al-Malik for building the Dome of the Rock (e.g., later claims about diverting pilgrimage) are **disputed and largely rejected** by careful historians; the building's role as a monument of faith and statecraft is the sound reading.

## What We Don't Know

- ❓ The exact internal politics and many specific events of the Second Fitnah — the sources are tangled and partisan.
- ❓ The full architectural and inscriptional intent behind the Dome of the Rock beyond what survives.
- ❓ The precise degree of personal responsibility of individual caliphs for the harshness of their governors.

## The Lessons

1. **Order rebuilt is a mercy — but the means matter.** Abd al-Malik ended a devastating civil war and gave the ummah institutions that lasted centuries. Yet how power is won and held is itself part of its account before Allah.
2. **A civilisation needs a shared language and shared symbols.** Arabising the administration and stamping coins with *tawhid* bound a vast, diverse empire together. Identity is built deliberately, not by accident.
3. **Honour the sacred places of the deen.** The care lavished on Jerusalem reflects a truth the Qur'an affirms: some places are blessed, and beautifying them is a form of devotion.
4. **Recognise good and name wrong without hatred.** The mature believer can admire the Umayyads' achievements and grieve their injustices at once — refusing both blind praise and blind cursing.

# EPISODE 75 — THE TIDE REACHES THREE CONTINENTS

## The Story

Under Abd al-Malik's son, **al-Walid I (r. 86–96 AH / 705–715 CE)**, the Umayyad Empire reached its widest extent — and it happened on **three continents at once.** In a single astonishing decade, the frontiers of the Muslim world leapt outward in three directions simultaneously, an expansion rarely matched in human history.

**Eastward, into India.** A young general named **Muhammad ibn al-Qasim**, barely seventeen by some accounts, led a campaign into **Sindh** (in present-day Pakistan) around **93 AH / 712 CE**. He is remembered in the histories of the region not only as a conqueror but, by many accounts, as a just administrator who guaranteed the religious freedom of the Hindu and Buddhist populations, treating them under the protected (*dhimmah*) framework. Islam's long, deep relationship with the Indian subcontinent — today home to one of the largest Muslim populations on earth — has one of its early roots here.

**North and east, into Central Asia.** The brilliant and relentless general **Qutaybah ibn Muslim** pushed across the **Oxus River (the Amu Darya)** into **Transoxiana (Ma wara' an-nahr)** — Bukhara, Samarkand, and beyond — opening the great cities of Central Asia, which would, within a few generations, become some of the most luminous centres of Islamic learning the world has ever seen. (It is no accident that the man we revere as the greatest hadith compiler, **Imam al-Bukhari**, came from *Bukhara* — a fruit of these openings.)

**Westward, across the sea into Europe.** This is the most famous of the three. In **92 AH / 711 CE**, a Berber commander named **Tariq ibn Ziyad**, serving under the governor of North Africa Musa ibn Nusayr, crossed the narrow strait from Africa into the Iberian Peninsula. The mountain where he landed still bears a form of his name to this day: **Jabal Tariq — "the Mountain of Tariq" — which Europeans render as Gibraltar.** With a modest force he met and shattered the army of the Visigothic kingdom, and within a few short years most of **al-Andalus (Spain and Portugal)** had come under Muslim rule. A famous tradition — *cinematic, but of uncertain authenticity* — holds that Tariq burned the boats behind his men and told them the sea was behind them and the enemy before them, so there was no retreat but victory or death. The story may be legend; the conquest was real.

The Muslim advance pressed on into **Francia (France)**, until it was checked near **Tours/Poitiers in 114 AH / 732 CE** by the Frankish leader Charles Martel — a battle European historians later mythologised as the "saving" of Europe, though the Muslim withdrawal owed as much to overstretched supply lines and internal strain as to the battle itself.

From the Indus River in the east to the Atlantic coast in the west, from the steppes of Central Asia to the Pyrenees — within roughly one century of the Prophet's ﷺ passing, *La ilaha illa Allah* was being recited across a span of the earth no single empire had ever governed.

## The Qur'an Says

- **Surah At-Tawbah (9:33)** — "*It is He who has sent His Messenger with guidance and the religion of truth to manifest it over all religion...*" — the spread of the deen the Companions and their successors witnessed across continents. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Al-Baqarah (2:256)** — "*There shall be no compulsion in religion; the right course has become clear from the wrong...*" — the principle behind the protected (*dhimmah*) status extended to Hindus, Buddhists, Christians, and Jews under Muslim rule. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Al-Mumtahanah (60:8)** — "*Allah does not forbid you from those who do not fight you because of religion and do not expel you from your homes — from being righteous toward them and acting justly toward them...*" — the Qur'anic basis for just and kind treatment of peaceful non-Muslims. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah An-Nahl (16:125)** — "*Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good instruction...*" — the manner in which the deen was meant to be conveyed, and often was, through traders and teachers. ✓ Qur'an.

## The Authentic Hadith

- **The deen reaching everywhere:** The Prophet ﷺ said, *"This matter (Islam) will reach what the night and day reach; Allah will not leave a house of mud or fur except that He will cause this religion to enter it..."* — **Musnad Ahmad**, graded **sahih/hasan.** ✓ Fulfilled visibly in this century of expansion.

- **Mercy in conquest — the prohibition on harming non-combatants:** The Prophet ﷺ forbade the killing of women and children in war. — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ The standard (however imperfectly individual commanders met it) against which Muslim conduct is measured.

- **The reward of the frontier:** *"Standing guard (ribat) for a day in the path of Allah is better than the world and all that is in it."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari.** ✓ The spirit that animated the frontier garrisons from Sindh to al-Andalus.

- **The conquest of Constantinople foretold:** *"Constantinople will surely be conquered; what an excellent leader will its leader be, and what an excellent army will that army be."* — **Musnad Ahmad** (graded **sahih** by a number of scholars). ✓ The Umayyads twice besieged it and failed; the prophecy would await another people centuries later (a thread for a future arc).

## Historical / Theological Context

**Three frontiers, three legacies:** The simultaneous expansion east (Sindh), north-east (Transoxiana), and west (al-Andalus) is historically remarkable. Each frontier seeded a civilisation: Sindh anchored Islam in South Asia; Transoxiana became a powerhouse of scholarship (Bukhari, later the great madrasas of Samarkand and Bukhara); al-Andalus became the dazzling western branch we devote all of Season 23 to.

**Conquest vs. conversion:** It is essential to be accurate. These were *military and political* conquests of territory and rulers — they did **not** mean forced conversion of populations, which the Qur'an forbids (2:256). In most conquered lands, the majority remained non-Muslim for generations and converted only gradually, often through contact, commerce, and the appeal of the faith — not the sword. The *dhimmah* system, for its time, offered protected status and religious autonomy that was frequently preferable to the rule it replaced.

**Tours/Poitiers in perspective:** The European framing of 732 CE as the battle that "saved Christendom" is a later romanticisation. Modern historians generally see the Muslim presence in France as a raiding frontier that was never a sustained occupation, and the withdrawal as driven significantly by logistics and the internal strains that would soon topple the Umayyads themselves.

**Sunni–Shia note (respectful):** The conquests were carried out under Umayyad authority and remembered across traditions as part of the early spread of Islam. We present them factually, honouring the just conduct where it occurred and not glossing over abuses where they occurred, and we **insult no people or nation.**

## Weak & Disputed Narrations

- ⚠️ **Tariq ibn Ziyad's "burning of the boats" speech** is a stirring tradition but of **uncertain/late authenticity**; it should be told as legend, not established fact.

- ⚠️ The young age of Muhammad ibn al-Qasim and several dramatic episodes of the Sindh campaign come from sources of mixed reliability; the *conquest and his reputation for relatively just administration* are well-attested, fine details less so.

- ⚠️ Hadiths specifically promising reward for the "conquest of India" (*Ghazwat al-Hind*) exist but are graded **weak** by many critics; do not build claims on them. ⚠️

- ✗ Triumphalist or hateful narratives (from any side) that paint these conquests as campaigns of forced conversion or wholesale slaughter are **historically false** and contradict both the sources and the Qur'an's own principles.

## What We Don't Know

- ❓ The exact troop numbers and many specifics of each campaign (the sources exaggerate).
- ❓ The true pace and mechanics of conversion in the conquered lands — clearly gradual, but the details are debated.
- ❓ Muhammad ibn al-Qasim's full biography and the precise circumstances of his later recall and death.

## The Lessons

1. **A faith carried with justice spreads farther than one carried only by force.** Where Muslim rule offered fairness and protection, it took root and lasted; where it was merely harsh, it faded. Character outlasts conquest.
2. **Knowledge follows the opening of doors.** The frontiers of one generation became the universities of the next — Bukhara and Samarkand turned from battlegrounds into beacons. Plant in soil you may never see flower.
3. **Beware the myths others build around your history — and the ones you build yourself.** Both "Tours saved Europe" and "Tariq burned the boats" are reminders to separate stirring legend from sober fact.
4. **No compulsion in religion (2:256) is not a slogan but a standard.** The integrity of the deen lies in inviting hearts, not coercing them — a principle to measure every era against, including our own.

# EPISODE 76 — UMAR II AND THE FALL

## The Story

Sometimes Allah sends, in the middle of a worldly age, a single soul who reminds everyone what the whole thing was *for.* For the Umayyad century, that soul was **Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz (R.A.)** — known to history as **Umar II** — who became caliph in **99 AH / 717 CE.**

He was himself a Marwanid prince, a great-grandson (through his mother) of Umar al-Farooq (R.A.), raised in luxury, the polished governor of Madinah. By worldly measure he had everything. And when the caliphate came to him — unexpectedly, by his cousin's bequest — he did something no one expected: **he wept,** because he understood it not as a prize but as a terrifying trust for which he would answer before Allah.

What followed was one of the most remarkable reigns in Islamic history, and it lasted barely **two and a half years.** Umar II tried to steer the entire empire back toward the model of the Rightly-Guided Caliphs:

- He **renounced the privileges of the throne**, returned wealth and estates that the ruling family had taken unjustly — starting with his own — and lived in austerity that stunned the court.
- He **reformed taxation**, removing unjust levies, insisting that non-Muslims who embraced Islam not continue to be over-taxed (an abuse that had crept in), so that justice, not revenue, governed policy. His treasury reforms were so effective that the famous reports say *there was scarcely anyone poor enough left to accept zakah.*
- He restrained the harshness of governors and reopened the channels of justice to ordinary people.
- He gave great energy to **knowledge**, and is widely credited with ordering the first official efforts to **systematically write down and compile the hadith** of the Prophet ﷺ, fearing that the death of the scholars would lose it — a decision of enormous consequence for the preservation of the Sunnah.

And he did one thing that healed a wound festering since the first fitnah. For decades, certain quarters had made a practice of **publicly cursing Ali ibn Abi Talib (R.A.)** — the cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet ﷺ — from the pulpits (*minbars*). Umar II **abolished this** disgraceful practice and replaced it on the pulpits with the recitation of the Qur'anic verse of justice: *"Indeed, Allah orders justice and good conduct..."* (Surah An-Nahl 16:90). In one stroke, a son of the Umayyad house honoured the Ahl al-Bayt and turned the tongue of the ummah from cursing toward the words of Allah. For this, and for his justice, many scholars have called him **"the fifth of the Rightly-Guided Caliphs."**

He died young — by the well-known reports, poisoned by those whose unjust privileges he had stripped away — and the empire he had tried to redeem slid back. The decades after him saw renewed internal strife, heavy-handed rule, and rising resentment, especially in the eastern provinces of **Khorasan**, where a movement gathered under the black banners, claiming to restore justice and the rights of the Prophet's ﷺ family.

In **132 AH / 750 CE**, that movement — the **Abbasid Revolution** — swept the Umayyads away. At the decisive **Battle of the Zab**, the last Umayyad caliph, Marwan II, was defeated, and the dynasty was hunted down. The age of Damascus was over. But — as we will see in the very next season — a single Umayyad prince would escape the slaughter, flee across an entire continent, and raise the banner again on the far western edge of the world.

## The Qur'an Says

- **Surah An-Nahl (16:90)** — "*Indeed, Allah orders justice and good conduct and giving to relatives, and forbids immorality and bad conduct and oppression. He admonishes you that perhaps you will be reminded.*" — the very verse Umar II placed on the pulpits in place of cursing. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah An-Nisa (4:58)** — "*Indeed, Allah commands you to render trusts to whom they are due and, when you judge between people, to judge with justice...*" — the creed of Umar II's reign. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Al-Qasas (28:83)** — "*That home of the Hereafter We assign to those who do not desire exaltedness upon the earth or corruption. And the [best] outcome is for the righteous.*" — the verse that describes a man who held the greatest worldly power yet sought no exaltation through it. ✓ Qur'an.

- **Surah Ash-Shura (42:23)** — "*...Say, 'I do not ask you for it any payment except love for [my] close relatives (al-qurba)...'*" — the love of the Prophet's ﷺ family that Umar II restored to honour. ✓ Qur'an.

## The Authentic Hadith

- **The renewer at each century:** *"Indeed, Allah will send to this ummah, at the head of every hundred years, one who will renew for it its religion."* — **Sunan Abu Dawud**, graded **sahih/hasan.** ✓ Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz (R.A.), who reigned around the turn of the first Islamic century, is widely regarded by scholars as the *mujaddid* (renewer) of that first century.

- **The just ruler in Allah's shade:** Among the seven whom Allah will shade on the Day when there is no shade but His is *"a just ruler (imamun 'adil)."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ The station Umar II strove for.

- **Cursing a Muslim is grave:** The Prophet ﷺ said, *"Cursing a believer is like killing him."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim.** ✓ This is *why* Umar II's abolition of cursing Ali (R.A.) from the pulpits was such a profound act of obedience and healing.

- **The merit of writing/preserving knowledge:** The Prophet ﷺ encouraged the recording and conveying of his words (*"Convey from me, even one verse"* — **Sahih al-Bukhari**). ✓ Umar II's order to compile the hadith was an act in this spirit.

## Historical / Theological Context

**"The Fifth Rightly-Guided Caliph":** This honorific, given to Umar II by many Sunni scholars, signals that the *standard* of the Rashidun never died — it could be revived by anyone willing to fear Allah and rule justly. His reign is the proof that the problem of the era was not the people but the *abandonment of the prophetic model of justice* — and that it could be recovered.

**The compilation of hadith:** Umar II's official directive (notably to the scholar Ibn Shihab az-Zuhri and others) to gather the Sunnah marks a pivotal moment in the preservation of the deen — the institutional beginning of the systematic written hadith tradition that would culminate, a century and a half later, in the great collections of Bukhari, Muslim, and the others (the subject of Season 20).

**Why the Umayyads fell:** Historians point to a convergence: the resentment of non-Arab Muslims (*mawali*) who felt treated as second-class; the unhealed grievances of the supporters of the Ahl al-Bayt; over-extension and internal feuding within the ruling family; and the organisational genius of the Abbasid movement in Khorasan, which united these discontents under the banner of restoring justice and "the rights of the family of the Prophet ﷺ." (As we will see in Season 22, the Abbasids' relationship to the Ahl al-Bayt proved more complicated than their slogans promised.)

**Sunni–Shia note (respectful):** Umar II is honoured warmly across the spectrum precisely because of his justice and his restoration of honour to Ali (R.A.) and the Ahl al-Bayt — a rare point of broad agreement in a contested era. We present this with gratitude and **insult no one,** including the Umayyads who fell, whose final accounting belongs to Allah.

## Weak & Disputed Narrations

- ⚠️ The **poisoning of Umar II** is the widely-transmitted traditional account but rests on reports of varying strength; that he died young after a brief reign is certain, the precise cause less so. ⚠️/❓

- ⚠️ Some of the most astonishing administrative claims (e.g., the exact statement that *no one* could be found to accept zakah anywhere in the empire) are likely **idealised summaries** of a real and remarkable prosperity, not literal census facts.

- ⚠️ Dramatic deathbed speeches and miraculous anecdotes attributed to Umar II vary in reliability; his documented justice and reforms are the solid core.

## What We Don't Know

- ❓ The exact cause and circumstances of Umar II's death.
- ❓ The full extent of his hadith-compilation order and exactly how much was gathered in his short reign.
- ❓ The precise interplay of factors that brought down the Umayyads — historians weigh them differently.

## The Lessons

1. **One just person in power can change everything — even briefly.** Umar II ruled for barely thirty months and is remembered for a thousand years. Impact is not measured by the length of your turn, but by what you do with it.
2. **True greatness is to fear the trust, not to crave the throne.** He wept at his own coronation. The mark of a worthy leader is dread of the responsibility, not hunger for the power.
3. **End the tongue of cursing; recite the words of Allah instead.** His replacement of cursing Ali (R.A.) with Surah An-Nahl 16:90 is a timeless template: when you find hatred on people's lips, give them justice and Qur'an in its place.
4. **The prophetic model is always recoverable.** No matter how far an age drifts, the path of the Rightly-Guided is never sealed shut. Renewal (*tajdid*) is always one sincere heart away.

## ✓ SEASON 21 COMPLETE

**What we witnessed:** From the grief of Karbala and the turmoil of the Second Fitnah rose the **Umayyad Caliphate** of Damascus. **Abd al-Malik** rebuilt the shattered empire, Arabised its administration, minted the coinage of *tawhid*, and crowned Jerusalem with the **Dome of the Rock.** Under **al-Walid**, the tide of the deen reached **three continents** — Sindh, Transoxiana, and al-Andalus — in a single decade. And **Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz (R.A.)** showed, in barely two years, that the justice of the Rightly-Guided was never lost, only waiting to be revived — healing old wounds by honouring the Ahl al-Bayt and beginning the written preservation of the Sunnah. Then, in 132 AH, the **Abbasid Revolution** swept Damascus away.

**The thread / theme:** *Empires rise on the strength of order and conquest — but they are weighed by a single measure: justice. And justice, once lost, can always be revived.*
